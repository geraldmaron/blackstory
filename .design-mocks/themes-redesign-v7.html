Redlining and the color line

≈ 9 min read Related themes

Q1 — 1919

On a July afternoon, Eugene Williams drifted across an invisible line in Lake Michigan off the 29th Street Beach. No buoy marked it, no ordinance named it — but the stones that killed him for crossing it were real, and so was the boundary they enforced.

Q2 — 1939

Twenty years later a federal surveyor unrolled a different kind of map on a Chicago kitchen table: the Home Owners' Loan Corporation's grading sheets, four letters — A through D — sorting neighborhoods by "risk" that tracked race more reliably than it tracked repayment.

Q3 — 1990–2024

Ownership on the South Side never closed the gap the grading map opened. Three census decades apart, the same tracts graded D in 1939 show homeownership rates roughly half those graded A or B two miles north.
