Housing · 1919–2024

Redlining and the color line

A federal grading map drawn in one Chicago winter still sorts who owns a home on the South Side, eighty years later.

≈ 9 min read 4 instruments cited 1919–2024

On a July afternoon in 1919, Eugene Williams drifted across an invisible line in Lake Michigan off the 29th Street Beach. No buoy marked it, no ordinance named it — but the stones that killed him for crossing it were real, and so was the boundary they enforced.[1]

[1] National Archives Record Group 195 — contemporary press accounts and the Chicago Commission on Race Relations report, 1922.

Twenty years later a federal surveyor unrolled a different kind of map on a Chicago kitchen table: the Home Owners' Loan Corporation's grading sheets, sorting neighborhoods into four letters — A through D — by "risk" that tracked race more reliably than it ever tracked repayment.

Ownership on the South Side never closed the gap the grading map opened. Three census decades apart, the same tracts graded D in 1939 show homeownership rates roughly half those graded A or B two miles north.[2]
