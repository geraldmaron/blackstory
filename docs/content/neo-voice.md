# Neo: the voice of BlackStory prose

Neo is the house narrative voice: precise, attentive to people, and readable without
specialist knowledge. It serves the evidence and the reader's question. It is not a
fictional witness, a substitute historian, or a reason to impose the same story on
unlike records.

This contract covers entity summaries, historical context, stories, chapters, Lives
narrative and captions. Interface labels, service messages and the site's own
explanations use [UI voice](../ui/story.md). Evidence review follows
[prose fact validation](../methodology/chapter-fact-validation.md). When elegance and
accuracy conflict, rewrite for accuracy. A style guide cannot certify truth.

## Part I: Purpose and register

Write history people can understand and check. Name the people, actions, places and
dates the evidence supports. Explain unfamiliar terms when needed. Distinguish the
historical event from the archive's record of it. Avoid generic claims about legacy,
resilience or significance when a concrete fact can do the work.

The register is calm, direct and humane. It need not be emotionally flat. Joy, loss,
ordinary life and uncertainty deserve appropriate language; none requires a staged
verdict, performed outrage or an instruction telling the reader how to feel.

Someone has to be telling this. A chapter that lists what happened, in order and with
every citation right, is a chronicle, and a reader can't connect with a chronicle. The
narrator's job is to explain why one thing led to the next, connect what the record
keeps apart, notice what a document does and doesn't say, and give a reader moments to
stop and feel the weight of a plain fact. None of that requires inventing anything. It
has to stay easy to follow and it can't be corny. Prefer sentences long enough to carry
a thought; the one-line punch is a tic, and short sentences earn their place only when
the content is short. Passing every rule below doesn't make a draft good. Avoiding
machine tells is the minimum, not the goal.

Fidelity, dignity, reader understanding, connection and craft are the priorities, in
that order. Dignity includes
agency where documented, but does not require turning every harm into a story of
resistance or recovery. Institutional silence does not make a person's history less
worthy, and an incomplete record is not a writing failure to conceal.

The earlier persona scaffold, compulsory narrative laws and rhetorical budgets are
retired. They invited selective research, invented connective detail and repeated
cadence. The research memos in `docs/research/neo-voice-foundations.md` and
`neo-profile-construction.md` remain background; their claims about persuasive effects
are not evidence that a particular draft is accurate or useful. Do not optimize for
reducing a reader's willingness to question the account.

## Part II: Structure follows evidence

Choose the opening that answers the reader's question. A direct factual introduction,
chronology, documented scene or explicit comparison may all work. A named person in a
place at a time is useful when supported; it is not a compulsory template.

For a record summary, establish identity and the particular contribution or event.
Preserve qualifying words even when space is tight. Historical context should add a
supported relationship, chronology or explanation, not repeat the summary with praise.
A short record can remain short.

For a longer piece, understand first, then write, then trace. Before drafting, name who
is reading, the question the piece answers, and for each section the one sentence the
reader should keep. Reorder before rewording. Write each section from your own account
of what happened and why, working from the closed fact base. Then trace every clause
back to it, and check that every name, number and date in the draft appears there. An
`allowedWording` field is the limit of what may be claimed, not text to reuse; only
quotations and contested scope stay exact. Don't assemble prose from claim sentences.

Give scenes room. Depth comes from fewer sections, not thinner ones: at a fixed length,
cut whole people and episodes before thinning every paragraph, and show what the
reader's missing where the omission matters. Every event needs what led to it and what
set it off. Ask the question the piece exists to answer, in the text, and answer it.

Decide what the reader needs to understand in what order. A scene requires sourced details; weather, gestures, private thoughts, dialogue and the contents
of a room cannot come from plausibility. Second person is optional and must not put
invented experience into a historical person's life or presume the reader's identity.

End where the evidence and question lead. A documented act of agency is one possible
ending. An unresolved dispute, a loss or a precise limit to knowledge may be the honest
ending. Do not search only for evidence that produces uplift. No obstacle requires a
matching route around it, and no story requires a present-day moral.

## Part III: Explanation and uncertainty

Give the context required to interpret a fact before or alongside it. Do not delay
qualifiers for dramatic effect. A statement such as “the first Black woman” must not
become “the first Black American” in a heading, card, share excerpt or mobile preview.

Material limitations belong in the visible sentence or immediately beside it. A method
note can explain sampling, definitions, source disagreement or missing records in more
detail; it cannot repair a misleading main statement hidden behind a collapsed control.
Don't manufacture false balance: explain why one reading is better supported when the
evidence permits that judgment. Retain unresolved disagreement when it does not.

Numbers need a population, period, unit and denominator where relevant. Natural
comparisons can help, but percentages and decimals are allowed when clearer and more
faithful. Do not round away a consequential distinction. Explain an estimate as an
estimate. A chart or caption must be defensible without the surrounding essay.

## Part IV: Sentences and diction

Use concrete nouns, precise verbs and natural variation in sentence length. Read aloud
for clarity, then read adjacent records to catch repeated templates. No fixed clause
count, rhetorical quota, mandatory question/answer pair or paragraph-length pattern.
Rhetorical devices earn their place by helping comprehension, not by making the voice
recognizable. Cut a flourish that adds an unsupported image or assertion.

Exact dates are not the rhythm of a story. Use the day only when it's disputed or does
work in the sentence; otherwise the month or year is often truer to how a person would
tell it. Keep method talk out of the middle of a scene: put a limit in its own sentence
at the end of the beat, or drop a figure the scene doesn't need, as long as the main
statement stays true.

Avoid:

- Generic acclaim and uplift: “trailblazer,” “indelible mark,” “against all odds,”
  “a testament to resilience,” unless a quotation or specific discussion requires them.
- Repeated conclusions telling the reader what the previous fact proves.
- Decorative scene-setting, invented motives, sweeping group claims and filler
  transitions. Editing out conspicuous words does not cure those underlying problems.
- Internal tracker IDs, workflow history, model commentary or site navigation posing
  as historical prose.

No em dashes in authored public prose. Use contractions naturally. Current
`findProseVoiceIssues` checks em dashes and selected expanded contractions, skipping
quoted testimony; Lives tests enforce those checks and article validation reports voice
issues. These are mechanical house-style checks, not an authorship or quality detector.
Do not change a quotation's wording to satisfy them.

| Use                                                             | Avoid as narration                                                  | Reason                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| enslaved person; person held in slavery                         | slave as a person's identity                                        | Name the person and imposed condition. Quote source terminology only when necessary and attributed. |
| self-liberated; escaped                                         | adopting “runaway” or “fugitive” from an enslaver's account         | Describe the action without adopting the record keeper's perspective.                               |
| the term the evidence warrants, with contested naming explained | automatic replacement of every historical term by a preferred label | “Massacre,” “riot,” “uprising” and “protest” make factual and interpretive claims; establish them.  |
| documented names, actions and roles                             | anonymous people used as atmosphere                                 | Do not invent names or agency where records do not preserve them.                                   |

## Part V: Truth mechanics

Every factual clause needs reviewed support, including incidental dates, career totals,
comparisons and connective context. The bibliography's relevance to the person does not
establish every sentence about them. Preserve claim-to-source mappings in the draft's
review record. Recheck after prose edits; a new qualifier can be a new claim.

- **Fact:** state it at the scope supported, with the surface's citation mechanism.
- **Interpretation:** identify the reading and its basis; attribute a scholar's argument
  and fairly consider substantial counterevidence.
- **Testimony:** name whose account it is and its context. Evidence that someone said
  something is not automatically evidence that the reported event occurred that way.
- **Judgment:** make ownership clear; do not disguise it as a measured or archival fact.
- **Connective:** a narrator sentence that only relates facts already in the passage:
  sequence, comparison, contrast, what the facts have in common, or what a record does
  and doesn't say. It needs no new source, which is a deliberate narrowing of the rule
  above, and it needs inference review instead. It adds no name, number, date, place,
  motive or feeling. If a careful reader would take it as cause, it's held to the
  causation rule below. Mark it `connective` in the evidence map, with the facts it joins.

Causal language follows [juxtaposition and causation](../methodology/juxtaposition-not-causation.md)
and [scholarship principles](../methodology/scholarship-principles.md). A before/after
sequence, nearby pin or correlated statistic does not establish a causal explanation.
A scoped historical explanation and a quantitative causal estimate need different
forms of evidence; neither can be created by a rhetorical verb.

Trace quotations and statistics to their underlying works where accessible. Preserve
locator, source context and significant contradictions. An official source can be wrong;
a community source can be best placed to know. Resolve by claim-relative evidence, not
institutional prestige or the number of websites repeating a statement.

## Part VI: Editing examples

These are illustrative edits, not uncited additions to the catalog.

| Failure                                                                                                | Better decision                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A reviewed claim says “first Black woman”; the summary says “first Black American.”                    | Restore the evidenced qualifier and reopen every derivative title or excerpt.                                          |
| A cited biography covers a coaching appointment; the writer adds a career title total from memory.     | Remove the extra total until its components are checked against evidence.                                              |
| A source gives a visiting appointment and later full appointment; prose calls the earlier year tenure. | Name the two appointment types and dates separately.                                                                   |
| “Despite everything, their spirit endured.”                                                            | Cut it. Add a documented action only if relevant and independently reviewed. Ending at the documented loss is allowed. |
| A caveat in a collapsed note contradicts an unqualified headline.                                      | Qualify the headline/main claim; keep the note for explanation.                                                        |
| Five summaries use the same obstacle, breakthrough and legacy sequence.                                | Reorganize each around its own reader question and evidence. Don't swap synonyms into the template.                    |

### What a person telling it does

The table above shows what to remove. These pairs show what to add. Each "after" uses
only facts already in its "before." They're models of a move, not sentences to reuse.

**Connect what the record keeps apart.**
Before: "On August 22, Hamer described the ride to Indianola and the jail at Winona, where she'd been jailed and beaten on June 9, 1963, on her way back from a voter registration workshop."
After: "On August 22, Hamer described the ride to Indianola and then the jail at Winona, where she'd been jailed and beaten on her way back from a voter registration workshop. So both stories she told the committee started with registering to vote, the first at a courthouse and the second at a workshop she was coming home from."

**Notice what a document does.**
Before: "Turner married Independence, an enslaved woman, on January 8, 1857, but their union was severed when she was sold."
After: "Turner married Independence, an enslaved woman, in January 1857. The House Historian gives that marriage one sentence, and the sentence ends with her being sold."

**Say what the facts have in common.**
Before: "He became the first African American elected governor of a U.S. state. Carol Moseley Braun entered the 1992 Senate race in Illinois and became the first Black woman in the U.S. Senate. Barack Obama won a U.S. Senate seat from Illinois in 2004."
After: "Douglas Wilder, Carol Moseley Braun and Barack Obama had this in common: a district line couldn't elect any of them. Each of those races had to be won across a whole state."

**Put two facts next to each other and let them work.**
Before: "A year before that speech he'd introduced H.R. 6963, which the House Historian describes as the first federal antilynching bill. It died in the Judiciary Committee."
After: "He'd written what the House Historian describes as the first federal antilynching bill, and on the day he stood up to speak it was going nowhere in committee and he'd already said he wouldn't run again."

**Tell it in order. Don't label it afterward.**
Before: "That day armed men overthrew Wilmington's elected city government in a coup, and Waddell became mayor. The killing of Black residents that day was a massacre."
After: "Black residents were killed in Wilmington that day and armed men overthrew its elected city government in a coup, and by the end of it Waddell was mayor."

**Loosen the date and let the sentence think.**
Before: "On April 15, 1929, the Speaker swore the House in en masse, a change that kept colleagues from singling out his seat."
After: "When the House met in April 1929 the Speaker swore everyone in at once, a change that kept colleagues from singling out De Priest's seat."

**Move method talk out of the scene.**
Before: "Contemporary Black Biography says more than 100,000 new Black voters by that November, and the Chicago Reporter, citing Dempsey Travis, says more than 230,000 voters were added to the rolls, figures that differ in window and definition."
After: "Nobody's count of that drive agrees with anybody else's, partly because they aren't measuring the same months or the same voters. The lowest figure here is the library's, nearly double its goal of 50,000."

**Know where the line is.**
Crosses it, invented interiority: "Hamer rode to Indianola knowing what it might cost her."
Crosses it, corny: "And so the granddaughter of a slave took her place in history."
Crosses it, implied cause: "After the Court ruled, the lines stretched for blocks."
Stays inside: "She went in first to try to register."

## Part VII: Self-check

Before review, verify that the draft answers its reader's question, every factual clause
maps to reviewed evidence, decisive qualifiers survive compression, and interpretation
is distinguishable from fact. Look specifically for invented texture, forced uplift,
repeated cadence, missing counterevidence and uncertainty hidden outside the main text.
Read the actual destination, including narrow screens and excerpts. Leave unsupported
material out and record unresolved research needs.

Then check the other direction. Is someone telling this, or are facts standing in a
row? Does each section leave the one sentence it was meant to? Does the piece explain
why things happened, not only that they did? Does every event have its buildup and
trigger? Is there a moment a reader would stop on? If you compressed it, which
documented human details went, and should they have? Inference and judgment should be
visible and present; a draft with none has skipped the work.

## Part VIII: Production loop and limits

Research → evidence review → draft → prose review → adversarial review → exact final
revision check → applicable validation → publication-role review. A factual change
returns to evidence review. Use a separate reviewer for consequential disputed claims;
self-review must be identified as such. Review is not permission to publish.

Reviewers name the defect and the limit of the supported meaning; the writer rewrites
the paragraph and it's rechecked. A reviewer's replacement sentence pasted into a draft
is how flow and accuracy both get damaged. Quotations, and wording a court or source
fixes exactly, are the exception. A true loosening from day to month or year needs no
new evidence review unless the day is disputed or does work in the sentence.

The three working playbooks are `blackstory-neo-voice`, `blackstory-prose-review` and
`blackstory-ringer-review`. Apply them to entity copy as well as longform. Use the
[skill review cases](../research/skill-review-cases.md) to challenge changes to this
contract; those are transparent development cases, not a held-out quality estimate.

Stories are stored in `reference.stories`; the story CLI's existing schema,
citation, packet and word-floor gates still apply where implemented. Chapter-format
content currently has a 2,000-word floor. That is a format constraint, not a research
method or proof of depth: choose an appropriate shorter format or propose a reviewed
gate change when evidence is insufficient, never pad prose to pass. Entity summaries
have no reason to inherit a chapter's length or commands.

Skill instructions are procedural controls. They cannot guarantee factual accuracy,
prevent every formulaic draft, or substitute for publication enforcement and observed
reader comprehension. Report what was inspected and what remains untested.
