Chapter 01 · The Count

A ledger open on his knee

1790 — 1870 · 14 min · 126 citations · 9 records behind this chapter

In the summer of 1790, a man walked up to a door in Philadelphia with a ledger open on his knee, and wrote down how many people lived behind it. He did not write down their names.1

The first federal census counted enslaved people as a number in a column beside the head of household. Free Black households got a line of their own, and a name — which is why, two centuries later, a researcher can stand on a sidewalk in the Seventh Ward and say who was there, and cannot do the same two streets over.2

By 1870 the column had a name in it. That is the year the census first recorded every Black American by name, and it is the reason Greenwood and a thousand places like it can be reconstructed household by household.3

What the ledger could not hold is the part this archive keeps trying to give back: that a number in a column was a person with a street, a trade, a church, and a reason to be standing where they stood.4

The nine records behind this chapter

A story is an argument built on evidence. Handing you one record at a time breaks the argument. This hands you the whole set, on the field, framed to fit — the handoff today's URL vocabulary cannot express.

The count is not the history. It is the shadow the history cast on a government form, and it is most of what survived.5
