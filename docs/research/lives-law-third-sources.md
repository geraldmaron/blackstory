# Third sources for the five sub-floor state-law records

**Prepared:** 2026-09-15
**Status:** Research only. Nothing in the repository was read-modified, no `bd`, no git, no database.
**Method:** Every document cited below was opened and read. Where the source is a scanned volume,
the volume's own OCR text or its page images were pulled down and the passage read in place; the
relevant passages are quoted. Nothing here rests on a search snippet, a catalog record, or an
abstract. Where something could not be opened, it is marked and named.

## What was found, in one line

Four of the five now have a genuine primary or state-government third source that was read end to
end. The fifth (Illinois) has a third source that is genuinely independent of the two on the
record, but the strongest document found for it turns out to be **the same scan one of the existing
sources transcribed**, and that is reported rather than hidden.

Three of the three open date questions are now answered: the Illinois 1865 repealing act's own
title and citation, the Massachusetts approval day (April 28, 1855), and the Oklahoma grandfather
clause's adoption date (the statewide election of August 2, 1910).

# 1. `ent_law_illinois_black_law_1853` — Illinois Black Law of 1853

Existing two: `teachinglegalhistory.unl.edu` (transcription), `chicagohistory.org` (museum copy).

## 1a. The independence problem with the obvious candidate

The obvious third source is the 1853 session-law volume itself:

> *General Laws of the State of Illinois, Passed by the Eighteenth General Assembly, Convened
> January 3, 1853.* Springfield: Lanphier & Walker, 1853, pp. 57-60.
> https://archive.org/details/generallawsofsta00illi
> (full text read at
> https://archive.org/download/generallawsofsta00illi/generallawsofsta00illi_djvu.txt)

I opened it and read the whole act. It is there, verbatim, and it settles the act's own dates:

> "AN ACT to prevent the immigration of free negroes into this state. *In force Feb. 12, 1853.*"
> … "§ 3. If any negro, or mulatto, bond or free, shall hereafter come into this state and remain
> ten days, with the evident intention of residing in the same, every such negro or mulatto shall
> be deemed guilty of a high misdemeanor, and for the first offence shall be fined the sum of fifty
> dollars…"
> … "§ 10. Every person who shall have one-fourth negro blood shall be deemed a mulatto.
> § 11. This act shall take effect and be in force from and after its passage.
> **Approved February 12, 1853.**"

**But it is not independent.** I fetched the UNL Teaching Legal History item page and it states its
own citation as "*General Laws of the State of Illinois, Passed by the Eighteenth General Assembly,
Convened January 3, 1853.* Springfield: Lanphier & Walker, 1853," and points at the Internet Archive
scan. Source 1 on the record is a transcription **of this exact scan**. Adding the scan adds
authority but not a second independent witness. Say so plainly; do not count it as source three.

*Correction worth carrying anyway:* the act runs **pp. 57-60**, not p. 59. The volume's own index
reads "Negroes, prevent emigration of, into state, 57 / sold at public auction, 57 / second
prosecution, 58" and "Mulatto, who to be deemed, 60." UNL's "59" is the Internet Archive leaf
number, not the printed page.

## 1b. The genuine third source: the 1865 repealing act's own volume

> *Public Laws of the State of Illinois, Passed by the Twenty-Fourth General Assembly, Convened
> January 2, 1865.* Springfield: Baker & Phillips, Printers, 1865, p. 105.
> https://archive.org/details/lawsofstateofillpub1865illi
> (full text read at
> https://archive.org/download/lawsofstateofillpub1865illi/lawsofstateofillpub1865illi_djvu.txt)

**What it establishes, verbatim.** The repealing act in full, at p. 105:

> "AN ACT to repeal section sixteen (16) of division III, chapter XXX of the Revised Statutes, all
> of chapter LXXIV of said Revised Statutes, and an act entitled 'An act to prevent the immigration
> of free negroes into this state,' commonly known as the 'Black Laws.' *In force Feb. 7, 1865.*
>
> Section 1. Be it enacted by the People of the State of Illinois, represented in the General
> Assembly, That section sixteen (16,) division III, chapter XXX, of the Revised Statutes of this
> state, entitled 'Criminal Jurisprudence,' and chapter LXXIV of said Revised Statutes, entitled
> 'Negroes and Mulattoes,' and an act of the general assembly of this state, **approved February 12,
> 1853**, entitled 'An act to prevent the immigration of free negroes into this state,' be and the
> same are hereby repealed; also section 23, chapter XL, Revised Statutes, entitled 'Evidence and
> Depositions.'
>
> § 2. This act to be in force from and after its passage.
>
> **Approved February 7, 1865.**"

The volume's table of contents lists it under "REVISED STATUTES" at p. 105, and the index carries
"Mulattoes and negroes. See Black Laws" and "Negroes and mulatoes. See Black Laws."

**Why independent.** This is a different volume, printed by a different printer (Baker & Phillips,
not Lanphier & Walker), for a different General Assembly twelve years later, digitized from a
different library's copy (Western Illinois University, CARLI) than the 1853 volume (Lincoln
Financial Foundation / Indiana State Library). Neither existing source cites it: UNL's item is the
1853 act only, and the Chicago History Museum page gives the February 7, 1865 date with no
session-law citation at all. And it is an *independent witness to the 1853 act itself*: the 1865
legislature recites the earlier act's exact title and its approval date of February 12, 1853, in
its own enacted text.

**Tier:** 1. The session law itself, in the state's own published volume.

**Dates settled:** Yes — **the gap the packet flagged (GAPS item 2) is closed.** The 1865 repealing
act now has a title, a session-law citation (*Public Laws of Illinois 1865*, p. 105) and a confirmed
approval date of February 7, 1865, from the act's own printed text rather than from museum web copy.

**Also worth noting:** the repeal was broader than the record currently says. It struck not only the
1853 immigration act but also chapter LXXIV of the Revised Statutes ("Negroes and Mulattoes"),
section 16 of division III of chapter XXX ("Criminal Jurisprudence") and section 23 of chapter XL
("Evidence and Depositions") — the testimony bar. That is a real fact about the in-force window's
end, and it came out of the primary.

# 2. `ent_law_new_york_constitution_1821_article_ii` — NY 1821 $250 freehold

Existing two: a Wikisource transcription, and Liebman in the *Albany Government Law Review*.

## Candidate third source

> *Reports of the Proceedings and Debates of the Convention of 1821, Assembled for the Purpose of
> Amending the Constitution of the State of New-York: Containing All the Official Documents
> Relating to the Subject, and Other Valuable Matter.* Reported by Nathaniel H. Carter and William
> L. Stone. Albany: E. and E. Hosford, 1821. Amended constitution as finally passed and signed,
> Article Second § I at p. **661**; concluding provisions and attestation at pp. **666-67**.
> https://archive.org/details/reportsofprocee00newy
> (full text read at
> https://archive.org/download/reportsofprocee00newy/reportsofprocee00newy_djvu.txt)

**What it establishes, verbatim.** The volume prints the constitution twice — once as reported by
the committee of revision (p. 638), and then, after the roll-call on final passage ("the question on
the final passage of the revised constitution as engrossed was then decided in the affirmative…
AYES — 98 … NOES — 9. The said engrossed constitution was thereupon signed…"), the adopted text at
pp. 658 ff. The adopted Article Second reads:

> "ARTICLE SECOND. Sec. I. Every male citizen, of the age of twenty-one years, who shall have been
> an inhabitant of this state one year preceding any election … shall be entitled to vote in the
> town or ward where he actually resides, and not elsewhere, for all officers that now are, or
> hereafter may be, elective by the people: **but no man of colour, unless he shall have been for
> three years a citizen of this state, and for one year next preceding any election, shall be seized
> and possessed of a freehold estate of the value of two hundred and fifty dollars, over and above
> all debts and incumbrances charged thereon; and shall have been actually rated, and paid a tax
> thereon, shall be entitled to vote at such election. And no person of colour shall be subject to
> direct taxation, unless he shall be seised and possessed of such real estate as aforesaid.**"

It also carries the in-force clause the packet says rests only on Wikisource:

> "Sec. I. This constitution shall be in force, from the last day of December, in the year one
> thousand eight hundred and twenty-two."

and the attestation:

> "DONE in Convention, at the capitol, in the city of Albany, the tenth day of November, in the year
> one thousand eight hundred and twenty-one, and of the Independence of the United States of
> America, the forty-sixth."

followed by the signature list headed "WHO SIGNED THE AMENDED CONSTITUTION," beginning "DANIEL D.
TOMPKINS, President."

**A substantive find, not just corroboration.** The volume shows that the clause *changed* during the
convention. The version the committee of the whole passed on the floor (printed at p. 43064 of the
OCR, in the proceedings) reads "…freehold estate of the value of two hundred and fifty dollars, over
and above all debts and incumbrances charged thereon, shall be entitled to vote…" — **without** the
"and shall have been actually rated, and paid a tax thereon" requirement. That phrase appears only
in the engrossed, adopted text. Anyone quoting the clause must quote the adopted version; the
packet's quotation is the adopted one and is correct.

**Why independent of the two on the record.** It is an 1821 imprint, so it cannot derive from a 2020s
Wikisource transcription, and Liebman quotes the clause from Dougherty and from Elwood's 1846
edition, neither of which is this volume. I also opened the Wikisource page: it gives **no source
note at all** for its transcription, mentioning only "Lincoln I:613-756" as a place where the
constitution is discussed — i.e. its chain runs through Lincoln's 1906 *Constitutional History of
New York*, not through the convention's own report. Honest caveat: all three sources describe one
underlying document, so this is authority independence rather than evidentiary independence of the
kind you get from two unrelated witnesses to a fact. That is the most that can exist for a
constitutional text.

**Tier:** 1-2. Not a session law, but the convention's own published proceedings printing the
engrossed text it signed, from the enacting body, in the year of enactment.

**Dates settled:** It removes the packet's GAPS item 6 caveat for New York: the December 31, 1822
in-force date and the November 10, 1821 attestation no longer rest on the Wikisource transcription
alone.

# 3. `ent_law_massachusetts_school_integration_act_1855` — 1855 school integration act

Existing two: `bc.edu` (Boston College Law Library), `massmoments.org` (Mass Humanities).

## Third source — the session law itself, page images

> *Acts and Resolves Passed by the General Court of Massachusetts, in the Year 1855*, ch. 256,
> pp. **674-675**. Boston: Secretary of the Commonwealth. Digitized by the State Library of
> Massachusetts, DSpace item handle 2452/97079.
> Item: https://hdl.handle.net/2452/97079
> → https://archives.lib.state.ma.us/entities/archivalmaterial/de646585-121f-4d6a-8c30-33172b37b6cd
> Page images (PDF, 2 pp.):
> https://archives.lib.state.ma.us/server/api/core/bitstreams/2b97275e-f814-4ead-b105-29b3f0cfcdd0/content
> Library's transcription:
> https://archives.lib.state.ma.us/server/api/core/bitstreams/c2b5be91-80c7-4cb5-859f-de781be75727/content

I downloaded the PDF and read both page images, and separately read the library's transcription.
They agree.

**What it establishes.** First, the act's real title, which the record does not currently carry:

> "**Chap. 256. An Act in amendment of 'An Act concerning Public Schools,' passed March
> twenty-fifth, eighteen hundred and forty-five.**"

Second, §§ 1 and 2 exactly as the record has them, plus **two sections the record is missing**:

> "SECT. 3. In filing interrogatories for discovery in any such action, the plaintiff may examine
> any number of the school committee, or any other officer of the defendant city or town, in the
> same manner as if he were a party to the suit.
>
> SECT. 4. Every person belonging to the school committee, under whose rules or directions any child
> shall be excluded from such school, and every teacher of any such school, shall, on application by
> the parent or guardian of any such child, state in writing the grounds and reasons of such
> exclusion."

Third, and this is the gap:

> "SECT. 5. This act shall take effect from and after the first day of September next.
> **[Approved by the Governor, April 28, 1855.]**"

**Dates settled:** Yes, both. **Approved April 28, 1855** — the date the packet flagged as unconfirmed
(GAPS item 4) — read off the printed page image, in the volume's own bracketed approval line, with
the same line visible on chapters 254 and 255 on the same page. And the effective date of September
1, 1855 is now sourced to the act's own § 5 rather than to a lesson plan.

**Why independent.** This is the official session-law volume published by the Secretary of the
Commonwealth, digitized and served by the State Library of Massachusetts — a different institution
from Boston College Law Library and from Mass Humanities, and a different artifact from either.
Mass Moments reproduces the text of ch. 256 in a teacher resource; BC's page is a narrative about
Robert Morris. Neither is the volume, neither gives the approval date, and neither carries §§ 3-4.

**Tier:** 1, with the state-library custodianship of tier 2 layered on top. This is the best answer
available for this record.

**Bonus find:** the packet's GAPS item 4 said the 1855 volume "is not on the Internet Archive under a
title search." That is true, and it is why it was missed: the volume is not on the Internet Archive,
it is in the Massachusetts State Library's own DSpace, chapter by chapter, one item per act. The
whole 1692-1959 run is there the same way.

# 4. `ent_law_oklahoma_senate_bill_one_1907` — Separate Coach Law

Existing two: Library of Congress *U.S. Reports* PDF of *McCabe*, and `okhistory.org`.

## Third source — the state's own printed act, in full

> *Emergency Laws Passed by First Legislature, State of Oklahoma, 1907-1908.* Ardmore, Okla.:
> Ardmoreite Press, 1908. **Senate Bill Number 1, pp. 7-12.**
> https://archive.org/details/emergencylawspas1908okla
> (full text read at
> https://archive.org/download/emergencylawspas1908okla/emergencylawspas1908okla_djvu.txt)
> Digitized from the University of Pittsburgh Library System's copy.

I read all twelve sections and the signature block.

**What it establishes, verbatim.** The act's own full title, which the record does not have:

> "**AN ACT** Entitled an Act to promote the comfort of passengers on railroads, street cars, urban,
> suburban, interurban cars, and at railway stations, requiring all railway companies, street cars,
> urban, suburban, interurban car companies carrying passengers on their trains or cars in the
> State, to provide equal but separate accommodations for the white and negro races, by providing
> separate coaches or compartments, and separate waiting rooms at stations and depots, so as to
> secure separate accomodations; … to provide penalties for the violation of this Act, and declaring
> an emergency."
>
> "BY MR. GRAHAM OF THE SENATE, AND MR. SKEEN OF THE HOUSE."

Section 1, which is what *McCabe* paraphrases:

> "Section 1. That every railway company, urban or suburban car company, street car or interurban
> car or railway company, lessee, manager or receiver thereof doing business in this State as a
> common carrier of passengers for hire, shall provide separate coaches or compartments as
> hereinafter provided, for the accommodation of the white and negro races, which separate coaches
> or cars shall be equal in all points of comfort and convenience."

Section 2, the waiting rooms, with the signage requirement the record does not carry:

> "Each waiting room shall bear in a conspicuous place, words in plain letters indicating the race
> for which it is set apart. It shall be unlawful for any person to use, occupy or remain in any
> waiting room, toilet room, or at any water tank in any passenger depot in this State, set apart to
> a race to which he does not belong."

Section 3, the definition:

> "The term negro, as used herein, includes every person of African descent, as defined by the
> Constitution."

Section 7, the clause the Supreme Court found discriminatory:

> "Provided, that nothing herein contained shall be construed to prevent railway companies in this
> State from hauling sleeping cars, dining or chair cars attached to their trains, to be used
> exclusively by either white or negro passengers, separately, but not jointly."

And the closing, which settles the whole in-force window from the act's own face:

> "Section 12. An emergency exists for the preservation of the public safety, by reason whereof this
> Act shall take effect **sixty days from and after its passage and approval.**
>
> GEORGE W. BELLAMY, President of the Senate.
> WM. H. MURRAY, Speaker of the House.
> **Approved December 18, 1907.**
> C. N. HASKELL, Governor of the State of Oklahoma.
> (SEAL) Attest: BILL CROSS, Secretary of State, By LEO MEYER, Deputy."

The penalties the record cites also check out from the text: § 5, "not less than one hundred nor
more than one thousand dollars," each trip a separate offense; § 6, "not less than five nor more
than twenty-five dollars" for a passenger. § 10 adds a penalty the record does not have: a conductor
who knowingly refuses to remove a passenger is guilty of a misdemeanor, fined fifty to five hundred
dollars.

**Why independent.** It is the enacted statute printed by the state in 1908, three years before any
federal court touched it and a century before the Oklahoma Historical Society's encyclopedia entry.
It is a different publisher, a different date, and a different genre from both existing sources, and
it is digitized from a university copy with no relationship to either.

**Tier:** 1. The session law itself in the state's own published volume.

**Dates settled:** It independently confirms both dates the record depends on *McCabe* for —
approval December 18, 1907, and effect sixty days after — from the act's own signature block and
§ 12, rather than from a court's later description. Sixty days from December 18, 1907 is
**February 16, 1908**, which matches the packet's estimate.

**One thing to fix on the record.** The packet reads *McCabe*'s "The act was to take effect sixty
days after its approval (§ 12)" as an ordinary delayed effective date. The section is actually
headed by an emergency declaration — "An emergency exists for the preservation of the public
safety" — and the act's title says "declaring an emergency," which is why the act appears in the
*Emergency Laws* compilation at all. The legislature used the emergency form and then set a sixty-day
delay inside it. Worth a line in the summary; it is a small but real fact about how the first
legislature moved.

# 5. `ent_law_oklahoma_grandfather_clause_1910` — 1910 constitutional amendment

Existing two: Library of Congress *U.S. Reports* PDF of *Guinn*, and `okhistory.org`.

Two strong candidates, both opened. Either alone clears the bar; together they cover both the text
and the date.

## 5a. The state's own court, three months after the fact

> *Atwater v. Hassett*, 1910 OK 299, 27 Okla. 292, 111 P. 802 (Supreme Court of Oklahoma, decided
> October 26, 1910). Oklahoma State Courts Network (the Oklahoma judiciary's official site).
> https://www.oscn.net/applications/oscn/DeliverDocument.asp?CiteID=3949

**What it establishes.** First, the amendment's own text, quoted by the court:

> "No person shall be registered as an elector of this state, or be allowed to vote in any election
> herein, unless he be able to read and write any section of the Constitution of the state of
> Oklahoma; but no person who was, on January 1, 1866, or at any time prior thereto entitled to vote
> under any form of government, or who, at that time, resided in some foreign nation, and no lineal
> descendant of such person shall be denied the right to register and vote because of his inability
> to so read and write sections of such Constitution. Precinct election inspectors having in charge
> the registration of electors shall enforce the provisions of this section at the time of
> registration, provided registration be required. Should registration be dispensed with, the
> provisions of this section shall be enforced by the precinct election officers when electors apply
> for ballots to vote."

Note the last two sentences: the enforcement machinery, which the *Guinn* quotation in the packet
does not include.

Second, **the adoption date**, in the court's own syllabus:

> "**Section 4 (a) of article 3 of the Constitution, an amendment adopted at the election on the
> first Tuesday in August, A. D. 1910**, is not invalid for the reason that it was submitted at the
> primary election held 'throughout the state,' at said time, and not at the general election to be
> held 'throughout the state,' for the election of state officers on the second Tuesday of said
> year."

The first Tuesday in August 1910 was **August 2, 1910**.

Third, **the article and section number**. The court calls it "**section 4 (a) of article 3**," not
Article III § 1 as the record has it. The record's citation should be corrected.

Fourth, how it got on the ballot, which the record does not say: "suggested by concurrent resolution
of the Legislature of the state of Oklahoma and proposed by an initiative petition on the part of
the qualified electors of said state, and afterwards adopted by a majority of the electors 'voting
at an election held throughout the state'"; and "the Governor issued his proclamation calling an
election for said date."

**Why independent.** A different court, a different sovereign, and a different publisher from the
U.S. Supreme Court's report; decided in 1910, five years before *Guinn*, so it cannot derive from
it. *Guinn* does not cite it. The Oklahoma Historical Society's *Guinn* entry does not carry any of
this. It is served by the Oklahoma judiciary itself, not a commercial or encyclopedic republisher.

**Tier:** 2-3. A court of last resort reciting the enacted text, on the state judiciary's own site.

## 5b. The Secretary of State's own election returns

> *The Oklahoma Red Book*, Vol. II, compiled by W. B. Richards, Corporation Record Clerk, under the
> supervision of Benjamin F. Harrison, Secretary of State. Oklahoma City, 1912, **p. 307**.
> https://archive.org/details/oklahomaredbook00okla
> (full text read at
> https://archive.org/download/oklahomaredbook00okla/oklahomaredbook00okla_djvu.txt)
> Digitized from the Library of Congress copy. The same table is in the duplicate scan at
> https://archive.org/details/oklahomaredbook0002wbri

The preface states its standing: "Being a publication by the state, it is intended that it should be
impartial and unprejudiced."

**What it establishes.** Under "MEASURES SUBMITTED UNDER INITIATIVE AND REFERENDUM," p. 307:

> "**INITIATED QUESTION SUBMITTED AUGUST 2, 1910.**
>  Vote. Majority.
>  Yes. No. Yes. No.
> **'Grandfather' Clause   135,443   106,222   29,221**"

and the note below the tables:

> "NOTE. — The only questions adopted by Constitutional majority were the suffrage amendment, the
> 'Grandfather' Clause, which is **amendment No. 1 to the Constitution**, and the Capital Location,
> which election was declared null and void by the State Supreme Court."

The same page shows the surrounding elections, which matters for reading *Guinn*: initiated
questions were also submitted June 11, 1910 and November 8, 1910. The grandfather clause was the
sole question on the August 2 ballot.

**Why independent.** Published by the Oklahoma Secretary of State, two years after the election,
from the state's canvass. It has no relationship to the U.S. Reports or to the Oklahoma Historical
Society. Honest note: the Internet Archive copy I read was digitized by the Library of Congress,
which also hosts the *Guinn* PDF — but LOC is only the scanning custodian here, not the publisher or
the author, and the two documents share nothing else.

**Tier:** 2. A secretary of state publication presenting the enactment record.

**Dates settled:** Yes. **The packet's GAPS item 7 is closed.** The amendment was adopted at the
statewide election of **August 2, 1910**, by 135,443 to 106,222. Two independent sources say so: the
Oklahoma Supreme Court in October 1910 ("the first Tuesday in August, A. D. 1910") and the Secretary
of State's own returns ("INITIATED QUESTION SUBMITTED AUGUST 2, 1910"). The record can replace
"before November 8, 1910" with the actual day.

**Still not established:** the date of the Governor's proclamation declaring the result, which is the
formal moment of adoption as distinct from the election. Neither source gives it. If the record
needs a proclamation date rather than an election date, that is still open — see the VERDICT table.

## What is still missing, and where to look

1. **Illinois — a third witness to the 1853 act that is not the Lanphier & Walker volume.** The
   Internet Archive scan is the same document UNL transcribed, so it is not one. The 1865 repealing
   act supplies an independent recital of the 1853 act's title and date, and that is the honest
   third source. If a fourth is wanted for the act's *text*, look for the *Revised Statutes of
   Illinois* chapter LXXIV ("Negroes and Mulattoes") of 1845, which the 1865 act repealed alongside
   it, or the Illinois State Archives' own copy of the 1853 enrolled act. `ilsos.gov` still refused
   the connection during this pass; a reader at the Illinois State Archives in Springfield or at the
   Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library can pull the enrolled act.

2. **Oklahoma grandfather clause — the Governor's proclamation date.** The Red Book gives the
   election and the canvass, and *Atwater* gives the election; neither gives the proclamation
   declaring the amendment adopted. Look in *Session Laws of Oklahoma 1910* or *1910-11*, which
   carry governors' proclamations on initiated measures. HathiTrust has both in full view
   (`uc1.a0001936731` for yr.1910 and `uc1.a0001936756` for yr.1910-11a, record 100143510), but
   **HathiTrust blocked every request from this session** — `babel.hathitrust.org` returns 403 to

## Two corrections the primaries force

- **Illinois:** the 1853 act runs pp. **57-60** of the *General Laws*, not p. 59. The volume's own
  index confirms it. UNL's "59" is an Internet Archive leaf number.
- **Oklahoma grandfather clause:** the amendment is **Article III, § 4(a)** of the Oklahoma
  Constitution, per the Oklahoma Supreme Court in *Atwater*, and "amendment No. 1 to the
  Constitution" per the Secretary of State's Red Book. The record currently says Article III § 1.
