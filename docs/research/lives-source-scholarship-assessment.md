# Can peer-reviewed scholarship fill the Lives gaps?

**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Question:** where the published census record has nothing, what does the scholarly literature
offer for occupation/class 1870-1930, urban residence 1890-1960, attainment/unemployment/income
1940-1960, and home tenure 1900-1920, and what would accepting it cost the method?
**Answer up front:** decline it as a source of figures. Its real value to this project is its
bibliography, and one narrow owner decision is worth making on the back of that.

---

## Sources opened

Every item below was downloaded and read. Table contents are what I saw, not what an abstract
claimed. Items I could not open are in the last section.

### 1. Collins and Margo, "Race and Home Ownership, 1900 to 1990"

NBER Working Paper 7277 (Aug 1999), published as "Race and Home Ownership: A Century-Long View,"
*Explorations in Economic History* 38(1), 68-92.
URL opened: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7277/w7277.pdf

- **Tables.** Table 1: white-black gap in male home ownership rates, by five-year birth cohort ×
  census year 1900-1990, with an average gap and separate white and black averages per year.
  Table 5: share of household heads and owners with mortgages. Table 6: black/white average
  property values, 1940/1960/1980.
- **Geography.** Table 1 is **national only**. Table 6 is the **four census regions**
  (Northeast, Midwest, South, West). Nothing at state level anywhere in the paper.
- **Underlying data.** (b) **microdata.** Table 1's source line reads "IPUMS (Ruggles and Sobek
  1997)"; Table 6's reads "IPUMS." 1930 and 1950 are blank rows because no IPUMS sample existed.
- **Granularity.** Fine on cohort, zero on geography. The four census regions cannot be summed
  into this project's six, and the South region alone merges Deep South, Upper South and Texas.

### 2. Collins and Margo, "Race and Home Ownership from the Civil War to the Present"

NBER Working Paper 16665 (Jan 2011), published in *American Economic Review* Papers and
Proceedings 101(3). The working paper carries the full Web Appendix.
URL opened: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16665/w16665.pdf

- **Tables.** Appendix Table 1: estimated owner-occupancy per 100 households, white / black /
  gap, for 1870, 1880, 1890, 1900, 1910, 1920, 1930, 1940, 1950, 1960, 1970, 1980, 1990, 2000,
  2007. Appendix Table 2: the same for all household types. Appendix Table 3: ownership split by
  farm and non-farm home, 1870-1910. Appendix Tables 5 and 6 repeat Table 1 for ages 25-34 and
  55-64.
- **Geography.** **National only.** Not one state or region figure in the series.
- **Underlying data.** (b) and (c) mixed, and the mixing is the point. The paper's own framing is
  that it uses "Micro-data Series (IPUMS) and published census volumes." 1900-1940 and 1960-2000
  are IPUMS 1/100 samples. 1870 is IPUMS with a hand-built proxy (real-property value plus the
  `NUMHH` dwelling-unit variable) because 1870 never asked about tenure. 1880 is a **chain of
  five inputs**: Ransom and Sutch's 19.6 percent farm-owner figure assumed to hold for the whole
  South, a non-farm rate averaged from an 1870 IPUMS estimate and an 1890 published figure, a
  farm-home share from the 1880 IPUMS, a white residual backed out of *Historical Statistics*
  series Da530/Da532, and finally a linearly interpolated 1.015 adjustment ratio. 1890 and 1950
  are transcribed from published volumes and then multiplied by interpolated adjustment ratios.
- **Universe.** The headline series is a "core sample": **male household heads, ages 25-64, in the
  labor force, not enrolled in school.** That is not the universe of any published tenure table,
  and it is not the universe this timeline shows conditions for.
- **Granularity.** One number per race per decade, nation-wide, on a non-census universe.

### 3. Margo, *Race and Schooling in the South, 1880-1950: An Economic History*

University of Chicago Press for NBER, 1990. Chapters read as separate PDFs.
URLs opened: https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c8792/c8792.pdf (ch. 2),
https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c8796/c8796.pdf (ch. 6)

- **Table 2.1, illiteracy in the South.** Panel A gives black, white and gap for ages 10+ in
  1880, 1890, 1900, 1910, 1920, 1930, 1940, 1950, plus males 20-64. Panel B gives black illiteracy
  by age and sex. **Geography: a 17-state "South"** named in the footnote (DE, DC, MD, WV, VA, NC,
  SC, GA, FL, KY, TN, AL, MS, AR, LA, OK from 1910, TX). **Data: (a) published tables for
  1880-1930**, cited to the page (1880, Census Office 1883, 919-25; 1890, 1897, 218-19; 1900,
  1902, 436-37; 1910, 1914, 1203; 1920, 1922, 1159; 1930, 1935, 235). **1940 and 1950 are (b)+(c)**:
  public use samples converted from years of schooling to illiteracy with assumed conversion
  factors, which is why those two rows are published as *ranges* (10.7-15.1, 8.9-12.0).
- **Table 2.5, per pupil expenditures on instruction, selected southern states, c. 1890 / 1910 /
  1935 / 1950.** Black, white and ratio for Alabama, Arkansas, Delaware, Florida, Georgia,
  Louisiana, Maryland, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia
  (plus Kentucky in the 1890 discussion). **Geography: state level.** **Data: (a) published
  federal and state government tables**, but not Census Bureau ones: Margo (1985) for 1890,
  Jones (1917) for 1910, Blose and Caliver (1938) for 1935, HEW (1954) for 1950. Margo's own
  contribution is deflating to 1950 dollars with the Warren-Pearson index, so the **ratio** is
  transcribed and the **dollar level** is derived. Tables 2.6 and 2.7 do the same for school year
  length and class size.
- **Table 6.2, distribution of employment in the South, males 20-64, 1900 / 1910 / 1940 / 1950.**
  Occupation shares by race across white collar, skilled, semi-skilled, service, unskilled
  non-farm, farm operator, farm laborer, with a segregation index. **Geography: the same 17-state
  South aggregate.** **Data: (b) microdata** — the source line reads "1900, 1910, census public
  use sample; 1940, 1950, 20 percent random sample of census public use tapes," and the table
  prints sample sizes (2,065 black men in 1900). **1870, 1880, 1890, 1920 and 1930 are absent**,
  because no public use sample existed for them.

### 4. Collins and Margo, "Historical Perspectives on Racial Differences in Schooling"

NBER Working Paper 9770 (2003), published as ch. 3 of the *Handbook of the Economics of
Education* (2006).
URL opened: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9770/w9770.pdf

- **Tables.** 1-3: illiteracy by birth cohort 1870-1930 for blacks, whites and the gap. 5-6:
  school attendance and enrollment rates. 7-8: mean and distribution of years of education by
  cohort. 12: black-white ratio of per pupil spending by southern state (lifted from Margo 1990,
  pp. 21-22). 13: returns to literacy by race and region.
- **Geography.** Panels split **all states / southern-born / non-southern-born**. Table 12 is the
  only state-level table, and it is Margo 1990's.
- **Underlying data.** (b), and the authors say so plainly: "we have relied very heavily on one
  source of data, namely the IPUMS." The 1890 and 1930 rows are filled from published volumes
  precisely because no IPUMS sample exists for those years.

### 5. Bayer and Charles, "Divergent Paths"

NBER Working Paper 22797 (Nov 2016, rev. Sept 2017), published in *Quarterly Journal of
Economics* 133(3), 2018.
URL opened: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22797/w22797.pdf

- **Tables.** Black-white earnings differences at the 10th, median and 90th percentiles, plus
  positional rank gaps, for 1940-2014.
- **Geography.** **National only.**
- **Underlying data.** (b) **microdata**, stated in the data section: decennial census 1940-2000
  plus ACS 2005-2014, men aged 25-54. Cells with no data are filled "by using data from nearby
  cells," which adds (c) on top.

### 6. Collins and Wanamaker, "African American Intergenerational Economic Mobility Since 1880"

NBER Working Paper 23395 (2017, rev. 2021).
URL opened: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23395/w23395.pdf

Read the abstract and front matter only, which is enough to rule it out: the authors "build new
historical datasets" of **linked individual census records**. That is microdata twice over, the
output is father-son mobility matrices rather than population shares, and there is no state
geography. Not usable here.

### 7. Jones, *Negro Education*

U.S. Bureau of Education Bulletin 1916, Nos. 38-39 (published 1917), prepared with the
Phelps-Stokes Fund. This is the source behind Margo's 1910 column.
URL opened: https://archive.org/download/ERIC_ED542634/ERIC_ED542634.pdf

- **Tables.** Teacher salary totals, children aged 6-14, and per capita, given three ways: all
  children, white children, Negro children, **by state** (Maryland, Kentucky, Virginia, Alabama,
  South Carolina, North Carolina in the summary on p. 23, with the full set on the Volume II
  state maps). A second table breaks 1,055 counties into five bands by Negro population share
  with white and Negro per capita in each.
- **Geography.** **State level, and county level in Volume II.**
- **Underlying data.** (a) published government tables, with the denominator taken from the 1910
  census enumeration of children 6-14. The per capita figure is therefore a ratio the Bureau
  computed, not a raw count.
- **Caveats that matter.** The salary appropriations are for 1911-13 against a 1910 denominator,
  so the "c. 1910" label is a range. The scan's OCR is poor and mangles digits, so double-entry
  transcription is mandatory, which the method already requires. And Jones himself was publicly
  attacked by W. E. B. Du Bois in *The Crisis* over this report's treatment of Black-controlled
  higher education. Using its numbers is defensible; using its framing is not.

### 8. U.S. Bureau of the Census, *Negro Population in the United States, 1790-1915*

Census monograph by John Cummings, GPO, 1918. **This is the most important thing I found, and it
is not scholarship.**
URL opened: https://archive.org/download/negropopulationi00unit/negropopulationi00unit_djvu.txt

Verified by reading the table headers and first rows in the text myself:

- **Table 9, p. 92** — "Negroes in urban and rural communities, **by divisions and states: 1910,
  1900, and 1890**." Urban and rural counts, percent urban and percent rural, and percent Negro
  of the total urban and total rural population, for all three years.
- **Table 13, p. 466** — "Home ownership **by divisions and states: 1910 and 1900**." Table 14
  does the same for **1890**. Table 12 gives owned homes of Negro families by division and state
  for 1900 and 1910. Table 18 goes down to counties for 1910.
- **Table 17, p. 517** — "**Negro males 10 years of age and over gainfully employed, by states:
  1910**," full occupational classification down the rows, every state across the columns. Table
  18 is the same for females; Tables 15-16 give divisions and states summaries.
- Chapter XX covers Negro agriculture including tenure classes; Chapter XVIII's p. 459 table is
  the black-family tenure series for 1890, 1900 and 1910 that Collins and Margo relied on.

Provenance is (a), a Census Bureau publication, which the current method already accepts. These
tables are Negro-only, so a white comparator has to come from the corresponding general volume,
and that join is where the care goes.

## What the pattern is

Every scholarly series I opened lands in one of two buckets.

**Bucket one: national or a multi-state aggregate, built from microdata.** Collins and Margo's
tenure series, Margo's employment-distribution table, Collins and Margo's schooling tables, Bayer
and Charles, Collins and Wanamaker. These are good papers. They are simply answering a different
question from the one this surface asks. A national figure fills one of seven areas and tells a
reader nothing about whether the Deep South differed from the Northeast, which is the entire
reason the six regions exist. And they are built on IPUMS, so importing them is the microdata
decision arriving through the back door wearing a citation.

**Bucket two: state-level and clean, but transcribed from published government tables the project
could transcribe itself.** Margo's Table 2.5 and Table 2.1 Panel A. Here the scholar's added value
is a bibliography and a deflator, not a measurement.

There is no third bucket. I did not find a single peer-reviewed series that is state level,
crossed by race, and built from something other than microdata. The reason is structural: a
scholar who wants sub-national detail before 1940 reaches for microdata precisely because the
published state tables are patchy, and a scholar who works from published tables ends up at the
same national and regional totals the Census Bureau printed.

## RECOMMENDATION

### The case for using scholarship

It is a real case and it deserves to be stated properly.

A blank cell is not neutral. A reader who moves through 1900 and finds nothing under "owns their
home" learns nothing, and may conclude the question was never asked, or that nobody has ever
worked it out. Both are false. Collins and Margo's 1900 figures (47.9 white, 22.0 black, a 25.9
point gap) are a careful, published, peer-reviewed reconstruction by two of the field's most
cited economic historians, and they are almost certainly closer to the truth than the reader's
guess. Holding to purity here means the timeline is more rigorous and less informative at the same
time, which is a bad trade if the purpose is a reader's understanding rather than the method's
self-image.

The method also already concedes the principle. `derived` figures include income bands estimated
by Pareto interpolation inside an open top bracket. That is a model. `lives-regions-and-sources.md`
already permits "a Tier-1 or scholarly source, opened and quoted" for count notes and rules. The
line between "we estimate" and "a named scholar estimates, and we cite them" is thinner than it
looks, and the scholar's estimate is usually the better documented of the two.

### The case against

The timeline's claim is unusually strong and unusually simple: **every number on this page is a
count somebody published, and you can go and check it.** That sentence is the product. It is what
makes the surface different from the hundred other places a reader can see a racial gap chart, and
it is what lets the project say "missing numbers never mean missing people" without flinching.

Three things break if scholarly estimates go in.

**The geography breaks first, and it breaks silently.** Nothing on offer is state level. A national
homeownership rate placed in the Deep South cell would be wrong, not approximate. Placed only in
the national baseline, it creates a timeline where the national row has values the six regions
that sum to it do not, which reads as a bug and defeats the region-sum validation that
`lives-regions-and-sources.md` names as the check on every ingested figure.

**The universe breaks next, and it breaks invisibly.** Collins and Margo's headline series counts
male household heads aged 25-64 in the labor force and not in school. The census tenure tables
around it count all households, and 1940's count occupants rather than householders, a break the
project's own table map already flags as real. Putting those in one column produces a line that
moves when the definition moves, which is exactly the failure mode the "a change is never computed
across two definitions without saying so" rule exists to stop.

**The provenance breaks last, and it breaks the promise.** The 1880 tenure figure is five inputs
deep: an assumption that a Cotton South sample holds for the whole South, an average of two
different years' rates, a share from a microdata sample, a residual backed out of a third source,
and an interpolated adjustment ratio. There is no cell text that honestly conveys that. "Estimated
by Collins and Margo (2011)" reads to a reader as a firmer thing than it is, and a reader cannot
tell it apart from the transcribed number in the row above unless the design shouts, and designs
that shout on every third cell stop being read.

And the replication test fails. Ransom and Sutch, Margo, Collins, Wanamaker, Bayer and Charles are
substantially one overlapping research network, acknowledging and citing each other. Collins and
Margo's series is the only long-run tenure series there is. That is not convergence, it is absence
of a competitor.

### Landing: decline, and leave the gaps as content

**Do not adopt scholarly estimates as figures. Do not add a third status beside `observed` and
`derived`.** The gaps stay `not_measured`, linked to the decade's count note, which is what the
method already specifies and what the plan of record already commits to in writing: "where the
count is silent, the page uses sourced narrative and records instead of estimates."

Instead, do three things with the gaps.

1. **Make the gap itself the content.** The reason there is no state-level occupation-by-race
   table for 1890 is that the census published occupation crossed by sex, age and nativity but not
   by race. That is not an accident of archiving, it is a choice about what was worth knowing, and
   it belongs in "What the count could see" as a sourced note. This is the strongest available
   answer and it costs the method nothing.
2. **Use scholarship where the method already allows it**, in narrative frames and count notes,
   opened and quoted, named in the text. "Collins and Margo estimate that nationally, about one
   black household head in five owned a home in 1900" is a legitimate sentence in a note. It is
   not a legitimate value in a cell. The difference is that the sentence carries its own author
   and scope, and a cell cannot.
3. **Harvest the bibliographies.** This is the highest-value action from this whole assessment.
   Collins and Margo's Web Appendix and Margo's footnotes are a page-cited map of the published
   tables the sibling agents are looking for.

### The gap-closing leads worth handing to the published-table lane

These are Census Bureau or federal publications, not scholarship, and they need no owner decision.
They came out of reading the scholarship's citations, which is the actual payoff.

| Gap | Lead | Verified how |
|---|---|---|
| Urban by race, 1890-1910, by state | *Negro Population in the United States, 1790-1915* (1918), **Table 9, p. 92** | Read the table header and United States row myself |
| Tenure by race, 1890/1900/1910, by state | Same volume, **Tables 12, 13, 14**, and the ch. XVIII table at p. 459 | Read headers myself; p. 459 described by Collins and Margo, who used it |
| Occupation by race, 1910, by state | Same volume, **Table 17 (males, p. 517), Table 18 (females, p. 521)** | Read header and first data rows myself |
| 1890 tenure, all races | Holmes and Lord (1896), the 1890 *Report on Farms and Homes*, pp. 167 and 175 | Cited with page numbers by Collins and Margo, who opened it. **Not opened by me** |
| 1950 tenure by race | Census (1953a) Table 2 for non-farm; Census (1953b) p. xxiv for farm, reported as nonwhite | Described by Collins and Margo. **Not opened by me** |
| School conditions by race, southern states, 1890-1950 | Jones (1917); Blose and Caliver (1938); HEW (1954) | Jones opened and verified; the other two **not opened** |

**Also record two traps the scholarship surfaced**, both of which would otherwise cost a session:

- **Two published census sources give different 1890 tenure counts.** The 1918 monograph's counts
  are uniformly higher than the 1896 Holmes and Lord report, more so for owners, implying 18.7
  percent black owner-occupancy against 17.5 percent. Collins and Margo state that the 1918 text
  never explains the adjustment, and chose the 1918 figures for blacks while keeping 1896 for
  whites. Double-entry transcription will surface this as a reconciliation failure; it is a real
  disagreement in the record, not a transcription error.
- **The 1943 Edwards volume will not solve the occupation gap.** Its race-crossed tables are
  national. Worth writing into the table map so it is not re-checked.

### The one source worth an owner decision

**Margo (1990), *Race and Schooling in the South, 1880-1950*, Table 2.5** (and 2.6, 2.7 with it).

Not because its numbers should be ingested. Because it is the only thing I found that is state
level, crossed by race, spans 1890 to 1950, and rests on published government tables rather than
microdata. And the decision it actually forces is narrow, cheap and answerable:

> **May a published *federal statistical* table be an `observed` source, or only a published
> *Census Bureau* table?**

The current rule says Census Bureau. Jones (1917), Blose and Caliver (1938) and the HEW 1954
survey are U.S. Bureau of Education and Office of Education publications. They are public domain,
state level, crossed by race, printed as counts, and checkable by a reader in exactly the way a
census volume is. The epistemic standard is identical. Only the agency name differs.

If the owner says yes, the project transcribes Jones, Blose and Caliver, and HEW **directly**, and
never needs Margo's number at all. Margo becomes the finding aid, cited in the methodology as the
scholarly work that identified the sources, which is honest and is what he actually contributed
here. Nothing new appears beside `observed` and `derived`.

**Why this one and not the others.** Collins and Margo's tenure series is national and microdata
and on a universe no census table shares, so adopting it means three separate compromises at once.
Bayer and Charles is national and microdata. Collins and Wanamaker is linked microdata with no
state geography. Ransom and Sutch is a manuscript-census sample of a sub-state Cotton South that,
in Collins and Margo's own words, "because it crosses state boundaries, is not readily mapped into
state totals," so it fails the geography test twice over. Margo's Table 2.5 is the only candidate
where saying yes buys state-level race-crossed data and costs nothing in provenance.

**What the surface would have to do if the owner says yes.** Less than it sounds.

- **Status:** unchanged. `observed` for a figure transcribed as the Bureau of Education printed it,
  `derived` for any ratio the project computes from two printed rows. No third status.
- **The cell:** unchanged in shape. If a dollar figure is shown it must be as printed, in the
  year's own dollars, never Margo's 1950-dollar deflation, because that deflation is his work and
  not a published count.
- **The citation:** names the agency and the bulletin, the way a census citation names the volume.
  "U.S. Bureau of Education, *Negro Education*, Bulletin 1916 No. 38 (1917), p. 23." Not "Margo
  (1990)."
- **The count note:** says that the salary appropriations are for 1911-13 against a 1910 census
  denominator, and that this is a measure of what states spent on schools, not of what children
  received.
- **The method doc:** one sentence in "Source and status" widening "a published Census Bureau
  table" to "a published federal statistical table," with the agencies named.
- **New condition:** school spending by race is not currently in the conditions list. Adding it is
  a scope decision separate from the source-rule decision, and the source-rule decision is the one
  that is cheap. They should be decided in that order.

## WHAT I COULD NOT VERIFY

- **Ransom and Sutch, *One Kind of Freedom* (1977).** I did not open the book. Everything I say
  about its Table 5.1 (19.6 percent of black-operated farms owner-occupied, drawn from a sample of
  manuscript records from the 1880 census of agriculture, covering a "Cotton South" that crosses
  state boundaries) comes from Collins and Margo's Web Appendix, where they describe using it. I
  downloaded the NBER historical working paper h0129, "One Kind of Freedom: Reconsidered (and
  Turbo Charged)," but it is a scan with unusable OCR and I could not read it. **Treat the
  characterization as second-hand.** It is consistent and from authors who plainly used the book,
  but it is not me looking at their table.
- **Smith and Welch, "Black Economic Progress after Myrdal," *Journal of Economic Literature*
  27(2), 1989.** Paywalled at JSTOR. The RAND book-length version (R-3330-DOL, *Closing the Gap*)
  returned HTTP 403 on every URL I tried. **I never saw an abstract or a table. Unverified, and
  excluded from every conclusion above.** If someone can reach it, the thing to check is whether
  any table is state level; I expect not, but I did not confirm it.
- **Farley, "The Urbanization of Negroes in the United States," *Journal of Social History* 1(3),
  1968.** Paywalled at Oxford Academic. This was my best remaining hope for the 1890-1960 urban
  gap and I could not open it. **Unverified.** Given the 1918 monograph's Table 9 covers 1890,
  1900 and 1910 at state level from a published census source, the marginal value of Farley is
  probably limited to 1920-1960, and even there he would be summarizing published tables the
  project can reach directly.
- **Blose and Caliver (1938) and the HEW 1954 survey.** Named as Margo's sources for the 1935 and
  1950 columns; I did not locate or open either. Their state-level, race-crossed structure is
  inferred from the shape of Margo's table, not observed.
- **Historical Statistics of the United States, Millennial Edition** (Carter et al., 2006). Not
  opened. I saw Collins and Margo cite series Da530/Da532 from Volume 4, p. 4-71 for 1880 farm
  ownership. Whether the Millennial Edition carries anything state level and race-crossed is
  unchecked; it is a Cambridge paywall.
- **Collins and Wanamaker (w23395).** I read the abstract and front matter only, not the tables.
  The ruling-out rests on the abstract's own description of linked historical datasets, which is
  sufficient for a geography and data-type verdict but is not a reading of their tables.
- **Volume II of Jones (1917)**, which holds the county-level state maps. I opened Volume I only.
- I did not attempt to determine whether the **1890 census published occupation by color at state
  level**. That is a published-table question and belongs to the sibling agents, but it is the
  single decade with no scholarly fallback at all, so it is worth someone's explicit look.
