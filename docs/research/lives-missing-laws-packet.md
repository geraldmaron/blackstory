# Missing Laws Intake Packet: "Lives Across the Decades"

**Prepared:** 2026-09-15
**Status:** Research only. No catalog entity was created, no database was written, no `research-intake` was run.
**Scope:** Seven federal laws and cases named in the bead, plus eleven state rules chosen to give the three uncovered regions (Midwest, Northeast, Texas & Oklahoma) a state rule in force.

## How to read the citations

Every load-bearing date and quotation below was read in the source named. Where a source was
fetched as a PDF, it was downloaded and its text extracted locally rather than summarized from a
search result.

Source classes used in this packet, strongest first:

1. **Statute text.** U.S. Statutes at Large page images from GPO's govinfo; state constitutions
   and session laws from the enacting state's own archive where reachable.
2. **Court's own report.** Supreme Court opinions from the Library of Congress U.S. Reports PDFs
   at `tile.loc.gov`. Cornell LII was not used for any quotation.
3. **Codifier's record.** The Office of the Law Revision Counsel (U.S. House) source credits and
   effective-date notes, used for amendment and repeal dates.
4. **Government archive or historical agency.** State archives, National Archives, state
   historical societies.
5. **Peer-reviewed scholarship.** Used for contested interpretation and for dates where no
   primary record was reachable.

Two sources count as one when they share a publisher or when one visibly derives from the other.
Where that judgment reduced a pair to a single source, it is said so in **GAPS**.

**One caution the packet earned.** The Library of Congress research guide page for
*Hernandez v. Texas* gives the decision date as May 2, 1954 and the argument as February 1954. The
Court's own report gives "Argued January 11, 1954.—Decided May 3, 1954." The U.S. Reports PDF
governs, and secondary government pages were not allowed to set any date in this packet.

# FEDERAL

## 1. Social Security Act of 1935

**Name as the record names it:** Social Security Act. Enacting body: 74th Congress. Chapter 531,
Public No. 271; 49 Stat. 620.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Federal.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Approved | August 14, 1935 | Statutes at Large caption, 49 Stat. 620 |
| Wage credits begin | after December 31, 1936 | Act §§202, 210, 49 Stat. 623, 625 |
| Monthly benefits begin | January 1, 1942 (as originally enacted) | Act §202, 49 Stat. 623 |
| Agricultural and domestic exclusions ended | January 1, 1951 | Act of Aug. 28, 1950, ch. 809, §104(a); effective date per §104(b), as noted by OLRC at 42 U.S.C. §410 |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The Act created a federal old-age benefit financed by a
payroll tax, and paid grants to states for old-age assistance, unemployment compensation
administration, aid to dependent children, maternal and child health, and aid to the blind. It
defined the covered workforce by listing occupations that did **not** count as "employment." Two
of those listed occupations, agricultural labor and domestic service in a private home, were the
occupations in which most Black workers worked in 1935. The result was that a worker could spend
a full career in covered-sounding work and accrue nothing.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** None. The Act contains no racial classification.
It classifies by occupation. The same two-line exclusion appears three times, once in each
operative title:

> "(b) The term "employment" means any service, of whatever nature, performed within the United
> States by an employee for his employer, except—
> (1) Agricultural labor;
> (2) Domestic service in a private home;"

That is §210(b), the Title II old-age benefit definition, at 49 Stat. 625. The identical pair
opens §811(b), the Title VIII payroll tax definition, at 49 Stat. 639, and §907(c), the Title IX
unemployment tax definition, at 49 Stat. 643.

**Life domains:** income_support, work.

**Posture:** facially_neutral. The text imposes no racial exclusion and names no race; the
occupational categories it excludes were disproportionately Black.

**Groups reached:** all by text; disparate effect on black. See the DISPUTES section, which this
record must render as a dispute rather than a finding.

## 2. National Labor Relations Act of 1935

**Name as the record names it:** National Labor Relations Act (Wagner Act). Enacting body: 74th
Congress. Chapter 372, Public No. 198; 49 Stat. 449.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Federal.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Approved | July 5, 1935 | "Approved, July 5, 1935," 49 Stat. 457 |
| Agricultural and domestic exclusion | never repealed; still in 29 U.S.C. §152(3) | NLRB statute text |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The Act gave covered private-sector workers a federally
protected right to organize, to bargain collectively, and to strike, and created the National
Labor Relations Board to enforce it against employers. The protection ran only to people the Act
called "employees," and the definition carved out agricultural laborers and domestic workers. A
farmworker or a household worker who organized had no federal remedy if fired for it, and that is
still true.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** None by race. Section 2(3), at 49 Stat. 450:

> "The term "employee" shall include any employee, and shall not be limited to the employees of a
> particular employer, unless the Act explicitly states otherwise, and shall include any individual
> whose work has ceased as a consequence of, or in connection with, any current labor dispute or
> because of any unfair labor practice, and who has not obtained any other regular and
> substantially equivalent employment, but shall not include any individual employed as an
> agricultural laborer, or in the domestic service of any family or person at his home, or any
> individual employed by his parent or spouse."

**Life domains:** work.

**Posture:** facially_neutral.

**Groups reached:** all by text; disparate effect on black.

**Note for the timeline.** This is the one exclusion in the packet with no end date. The NLRB's
current statute text carries the same clause the 1935 Congress wrote, with later additions for
independent contractors, supervisors, and Railway Labor Act employers.

**Citations**

1. *National Labor Relations Act*, ch. 372, 49 Stat. 449 (July 5, 1935), page images. U.S.
   Government Publishing Office, govinfo.
   https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-49/pdf/STATUTE-49-Pg449.pdf
2. *National Labor Relations Act*, current statute text including the §2(3) definition of
   "employee." National Labor Relations Board, accessed 2026-09-15.
   https://www.nlrb.gov/guidance/key-reference-materials/national-labor-relations-act

## 3. Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938

**Name as the record names it:** Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938. Enacting body: 75th Congress,
3d Session. Chapter 676, Public No. 718; 52 Stat. 1060.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Federal.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Approved | June 25, 1938 | Statutes at Large caption, 52 Stat. 1060 |
| Wage and hour provisions effective | October 24, 1938 | Act §6(b) ("upon the expiration of one hundred and twenty days from the date of enactment"), 52 Stat. 1062; date stated by U.S. Dept. of Labor |
| Agricultural coverage broadened | February 1, 1967 | Pub. L. 89-601, effective date per §602, as noted by OLRC at 29 U.S.C. §206 |
| Domestic service brought under the Act | May 1, 1974 | Pub. L. 93-259, Apr. 8, 1974, 88 Stat. 55; effective date per §29(a), as noted by OLRC at 29 U.S.C. §206 |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The Act set a federal minimum wage of 25 cents an hour
and a maximum workweek of 44 hours for workers engaged in interstate commerce or in producing
goods for it, and it banned oppressive child labor. Two separate mechanisms kept most Black
workers out. Section 13(a)(6) expressly exempted "any employee employed in agriculture." Domestic
workers were not exempted by name at all; they simply fell outside the interstate-commerce
coverage test in sections 6 and 7, so the Act never reached a household. The Department of Labor
records that the Act as passed covered only about one-fifth of the labor force.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** None by race. Section 13(a), at 52 Stat. 1067,
lists the exemptions; the agricultural one reads:

> "or (6) any employee employed in agriculture;"

Section 13(a)(10) additionally exempted anyone "employed within the area of production ...
engaged in handling, packing, storing, ginning, compressing, pasteurizing, drying, preparing in
their raw or natural state, or canning of agricultural or horticultural commodities for market,
or in making cheese or butter or other dairy products."

**Life domains:** work, income_support.

**Posture:** facially_neutral.

**Groups reached:** all by text; disparate effect on black. See DISPUTES.

## 4. Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 (GI Bill)

**Name as the record names it:** Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944. Enacting body: 78th
Congress. Chapter 268, Public Law 346; 58 Stat. 284.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Federal statute, administered through the Veterans' Administration's
regional and local offices, state employment services, private banks, and the admissions offices
of individual colleges.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Approved | June 22, 1944 | Statutes at Large caption, 58 Stat. 284; National Archives |
| Education benefit deadline | course to begin within 2 years of discharge or of the war's end, whichever later; no training beyond 7 years after the war's end | Act, Veterans Regulation No. 1(a), Part VIII, ¶1 |
| Loan guaranty application deadline | within 2 years of separation or 2 years after the war's end, whichever later; in no event more than 5 years after the war's end | Act §500(a), Title III |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The Act gave World War II veterans tuition and a
subsistence allowance for school or training, a federal guaranty of up to 50 percent (capped at
$2,000) on a loan to buy a home, farm, or business, unemployment allowances, and job placement
through the employment service. It wrote its eligibility test purely in terms of service: ninety
days on or after September 16, 1940, and a discharge "under conditions other than dishonorable."
It then handed the money to institutions that were free to discriminate. The VA guaranteed loans
but did not make them, so a bank's refusal or an appraiser's redlining of a Black neighborhood
ended the matter; colleges set their own admissions; the employment service ran through state
offices.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** None. A full-text search of the Act for
"negro," "colored," "white," "race," and "racial" returns nothing. Eligibility under Part VIII
runs to:

> "Any person who served in the active military or naval service on or after September 16, 1940,
> and prior to the termination of the present war, and who shall have been discharged or released
> therefrom under conditions other than dishonorable ..."

and under Title III, §500(a), to "Any person who shall have served in the active military or naval
service of the United States" on the same terms.

**Life domains:** housing, credit, schooling, work, income_support.

**Posture:** facially_neutral. The National Archives states the consequence plainly:

> "Black vets were often unable to get bank loans for mortgages in Black neighborhoods, and they
> faced prejudice and discrimination that overwhelming excluded them from buying homes in 'white'
> suburban neighborhoods."

**Groups reached:** all by text; disparate effect on black.

**Citations**

1. *Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944*, ch. 268, Pub. L. 346, 58 Stat. 284 (June 22, 1944),
   page images. U.S. Government Publishing Office, govinfo.
   https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-58/pdf/STATUTE-58-Pg284.pdf
2. "Servicemen's Readjustment Act (1944)." Milestone Documents, U.S. National Archives and Records
   Administration, accessed 2026-09-15.
   https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/servicemens-readjustment-act

## 5. Hernandez v. Texas, 347 U.S. 475 (1954)

**Name as the record names it:** *Hernandez v. Texas*. Court: Supreme Court of the United States,
on certiorari to the Court of Criminal Appeals of Texas. No. 406.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Federal; binding on every state.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Argued | January 11, 1954 | 347 U.S. 475 |
| Decided | May 3, 1954 | 347 U.S. 475 |
| Overruled | never | — |

Opinion by Chief Justice Warren. The report records no dissent.

**What the rule did, in plain language.** Pete Hernandez was indicted and convicted of murder in
Jackson County, Texas, by juries drawn from a county where no person of Mexican descent had served
as a jury commissioner, grand juror, or petit juror for twenty-five years. Texas argued that the
Fourteenth Amendment reached only two classes, white and Negro, and that Mexican Americans were
legally white and therefore had no claim. The Court rejected the two-class theory, held that a
distinct class can be shown as a question of fact from community practice, and reversed the
conviction. The decision was announced two weeks before *Brown v. Board of Education*.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** The opinion names "white," "Negro," and
"persons of Mexican descent." At 347 U.S. 477:

> "The State of Texas would have us hold that there are only two classes—white and Negro—within
> the contemplation of the Fourteenth Amendment."

At 347 U.S. 478:

> "When the existence of a distinct class is demonstrated, and it is further shown that the laws,
> as written or as applied, single out that class for different treatment not based on some
> reasonable classification, the guarantees of the Constitution have been violated. The Fourteenth
> Amendment is not directed solely against discrimination due to a "two-class theory"—that is,
> based upon differences between "white" and Negro."

At 347 U.S. 479:

> "The exclusion of otherwise eligible persons from jury service solely because of their ancestry
> or national origin is discrimination prohibited by the Fourteenth Amendment. The Texas statute
> makes no such discrimination, but the petitioner alleges that those administering the law do."

**Life domains:** justice.

**Posture:** protective.

**Groups reached:** hispanic directly; the holding is written as a general rule and reaches all.

**Citations**

1. *Hernandez v. Texas*, 347 U.S. 475 (1954), U.S. Reports page images. Library of Congress, Law
   Library.
   https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep347/usrep347475/usrep347475.pdf
2. "Hernández v. the State of Texas: A Landmark Civil Rights Case." *Handbook of Texas*, Texas
   State Historical Association, accessed 2026-09-15.
   https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/hernandez-v-state-of-texas

## 6. Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart-Celler Act)

**Name as the record names it:** An Act to amend the Immigration and Nationality Act, and for
other purposes. Enacting body: 89th Congress. Public Law 89-236; 79 Stat. 911.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Federal.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Enacted | October 3, 1965 | Statutes at Large caption, 79 Stat. 911 |
| General effective date | December 1, 1965 | Act §20: "the first day of the first month after the expiration of thirty days following the date of its enactment" |
| Western Hemisphere ceiling of 120,000 takes effect | fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968 | Act §21(e) |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The Act abolished the national-origins quota system that
had governed U.S. immigration since the 1920s and replaced it with a preference system built on
family reunification and occupational skills, a 20,000-per-country annual cap, and an Eastern
Hemisphere ceiling of 170,000. It added an express antidiscrimination rule for the issuance of
immigrant visas. Its sponsors expected the source countries of immigration to stay roughly as they
were; they did not. The House historians record that after Hart-Celler "annual immigration jumped
to nearly a half million people, and only 20 percent came from Europe."

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** The new §202(a), at 79 Stat. 911, names the
protected characteristics rather than groups:

> "No person shall receive any preference or priority or be discriminated against in the issuance
> of an immigrant visa because of his race, sex, nationality, place of birth, or place of
> residence, except as specifically provided in section 101(a)(27), section 201(b), and section
> 203"

**Life domains:** immigration.

**Posture:** protective. It removed a racial and national-origin allocation from federal law. Note
for the record's honesty: the same Act imposed the first numerical ceiling ever placed on Western
Hemisphere immigration, which restricted Mexican migration that had previously been unnumbered.
That is a real and documented second effect, and the record should carry it rather than present
the Act as purely liberalizing.

**Groups reached:** all; in practice the largest effects fell on immigrants from Asia, Africa, the
Caribbean, and Latin America, and so on hispanic and black populations in the United States.

**Citations**

1. *An Act to amend the Immigration and Nationality Act*, Pub. L. 89-236, 79 Stat. 911 (Oct. 3,
   1965), page images. U.S. Government Publishing Office, govinfo.
   https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-79/pdf/STATUTE-79-Pg911.pdf
2. "The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965." Historical Highlights, Office of the Historian
   and Office of the Clerk, U.S. House of Representatives, accessed 2026-09-15.
   https://history.house.gov/Historical-Highlights/1951-2000/Immigration-and-Nationality-Act-of-1965/

## 7. Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986

**Name as the record names it:** Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986. Enacting body: 99th
Congress. Public Law 99-603; 100 Stat. 3359.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Federal.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Enacted | November 6, 1986 | OLRC source credit at 8 U.S.C. §1324a: "Pub. L. 99-603, title I, §101(a)(1), Nov. 6, 1986, 100 Stat. 3360" |
| Legalization application window opens | a date designated by the Attorney General, not later than 180 days after November 6, 1986 | INA §245A(a)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. §1255a |
| Legalization window length | 12 months from that date | INA §245A(a)(1)(A) |
| Employer sanctions | still in force | 8 U.S.C. §1324a |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The Act made it unlawful for the first time for an
employer to knowingly hire a worker without work authorization, and required every employer to
verify the identity and work eligibility of every new hire. It paired that with a one-time
legalization for long-resident unauthorized immigrants. Anticipating that employers would respond
to the new penalties by refusing to hire anyone who looked or sounded foreign, Congress added a
new unfair-immigration-related-employment-practice provision covering national origin and
citizenship status.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** No racial classification. The antidiscrimination
section, INA §274B as added by §102(a) and codified at 8 U.S.C. §1324b, names:

> "It is an unfair immigration-related employment practice for a person or other entity to
> discriminate against any individual (other than an unauthorized alien ...) with respect to the
> hiring, or recruitment or referral for a fee, of the individual for employment or the discharging
> of the individual from employment— (A) because of such individual's national origin, or (B) in
> the case of a protected individual ..., because of such individual's citizenship status."

Note the exception written into §1324b(a)(2)(A): the provision "shall not apply to a person or
other entity that employs three or fewer employees."

**Life domains:** immigration, work.

**Posture:** facially_neutral. The Act carries a protective section (§1324b) inside a statute whose
main operative mechanism (§1324a verification and sanctions) creates the hiring-discrimination
risk that section exists to police. The record should show both rather than assign one posture to
the whole Act.

**Groups reached:** all by text; in practice hispanic most directly, and black immigrant workers
from the Caribbean and Africa.

**Citations**

1. *8 U.S.C. §1324a — Unlawful employment of aliens*, text, source credit, and notes. Office of the
   Law Revision Counsel, U.S. House of Representatives, prelim edition, accessed 2026-09-15.
   https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1324a&num=0&edition=prelim
2. *8 U.S.C. §1324b — Unfair immigration-related employment practices*, and *8 U.S.C. §1255a —
   Adjustment of status of certain entrants*, text and source credits. Office of the Law Revision
   Counsel, U.S. House of Representatives, prelim edition, accessed 2026-09-15.
   https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1324b&num=0&edition=prelim
   https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title8-section1255a&num=0&edition=prelim

## 8. Illinois Black Law of 1853

**Name as the record names it:** "An Act to prevent the immigration of free negroes into this
State." Enacting body: Illinois General Assembly, 18th General Assembly.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** State (Illinois). Serves the **Midwest** region.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Approved | February 12, 1853 | *General Laws of the State of Illinois, Passed by the Eighteenth General Assembly* (Springfield: Lanphier & Walker, 1853), p. 59 |
| Repealed | February 7, 1865 | Chicago History Museum; Illinois State Archives exhibit (see GAPS on this date) |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The Act made it a crime for a Black person to enter
Illinois and stay. A Black person who came into the state and remained ten days with the intent to
reside there committed a misdemeanor punishable by a fine. A person who could not pay the fine was
not merely jailed: the justice of the peace was directed to sell that person at public auction to
whoever would pay the fine and costs, and the purchaser was then entitled to the person's labor
for a set term. Penalties escalated for a second offense. It was the most restrictive law of its
kind passed by a free state.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** "negro, or mulatto, bond or free." The act's
own title names "free negroes." Section 3 provides that if any "negro, or mulatto, bond or free,
shall hereafter come into this state and remain ten days" with intent to reside, that person may
be prosecuted for a misdemeanor and fined fifty dollars; Section 4 provides that on nonpayment
"the said justice shall, at public auction, proceed to sell said negro or mulatto" to satisfy the
fine.

**Life domains:** family, work, justice, housing. The ten-day rule operated directly on residence
and on family reunification; the auction clause operated on labor; enforcement ran through
justices of the peace.

**Posture:** exclusionary.

**Groups reached:** black.

1. "An Act to Prevent the Immigration of Free Negroes into this State (1853)," transcription with
   session-law citation. Teaching Legal History, University of Nebraska-Lincoln, accessed
   2026-09-15. https://teachinglegalhistory.unl.edu/s/oer/item/1842
2. "Concert is Power: The Opposition to Illinois' 'Black Laws'." Chicago History Museum, accessed
   2026-09-15. https://www.chicagohistory.org/concert-is-power-illinois-black-laws/

| Struck from the constitution | 1881 amendments | Indiana Magazine of History; Indiana Historical Bureau |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** Indiana wrote a Black exclusion clause into its
constitution and asked voters to ratify it as a separate question, which they did by a wider
margin than the constitution itself. Article XIII barred any Black or mixed-race person from
entering or settling in the state, voided any contract made with such a person, fined anyone who
employed or encouraged them to stay, and directed the fines collected into a fund to pay for
colonizing Indiana's existing Black residents outside the state. An 1852 enactment required county
clerks to keep a Register of Negroes and Mulattoes.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** "negro or mulatto," "Negro or Mulatto,"
"Negroes and Mulattoes." The full article, from the Indiana Historical Bureau's transcription:

> **Section 1.** "No negro or mulatto shall come into or settle in the State, after the adoption of
> this Constitution."
>
> **Section 2.** "All contracts made with any Negro or Mulatto coming into the State, contrary to
> the provisions of the foregoing section, shall be void; and any person who shall employ such
> Negro or Mulatto, or otherwise encourage him to remain in the State, shall be fined in any sum
> not less than ten dollars, nor more than five hundred dollars."
>
> **Section 3.** "All fines which may be collected for a violation of the provisions of this
> article, or of any law which [m]ay hereafter be passed for the purpose of carrying the same into
> execution, shall be set apart and appropriated for the colonization of such Negroes and
> Mulattoes, and their descendants, as may be in the State at the adoption of this Constitution,
> and may be willing to emigrate."
>
> **Section 4.** "The General Assembly shall pass laws to carry out the provisions of this article."

**Life domains:** housing, work, family.

**Posture:** exclusionary.

**Groups reached:** black.

**How it ended.** The Indiana Supreme Court held in 1866 in *Smith v. Moody*, 26 Ind. 299, that
under the Thirteenth Amendment Black people were entitled to make and enforce contracts and that
the contrary provisions of the Indiana Constitution were unconstitutional. Governor Morton asked
the legislature in 1867 to wipe the dead provisions off the books; the constitution itself was not
changed until the group of amendments adopted in 1881, which struck the clauses banning Black
residence and voting. Article XIII was then rewritten to deal with municipal indebtedness, which
is what Article 13 of the Indiana Constitution is about today.

## 11. New York Constitution of 1821, Article II, Section 1

**Name as the record names it:** Constitution of the State of New York (1821), Article II,
Section 1. Enacting body: New York Constitutional Convention, ratified by the electorate.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** State (New York). Serves the **Northeast** region.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Adopted by convention | November 10, 1821 | Constitution's own attestation: "the tenth day of November, in the year one thousand eight hundred and twenty-one" |
| Ratified by voters | January 1822, by about 64 percent | Liebman, *Albany Government Law Review*, citing Dept. of State voting statistics |
| In force from | December 31, 1822 | Constitution's own text: "This Constitution shall be in force from the last day of December, in the year one thousand eight hundred and twenty-two." |
| Carried forward | Constitution of 1846 retained the same clause | Liebman, quoting I.R. Elwood, *Constitution of the State of New York, Adopted November 3, 1846*, at 8 |
| Superseded | 1870, Fifteenth Amendment | Liebman |
| Struck from the state constitution | 1874, by amendment approved by the electorate | Liebman |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The 1821 convention abolished the property qualification
for white men and imposed one on Black men in the same sentence. A white man could vote if he had
paid a tax or served in the militia. A Black man had to have been a citizen of the state for three
years and to have owned, for a year, a debt-free freehold worth $250 on which he had actually been
rated and paid tax. The effect was not a marginal narrowing; it removed the vote from nearly every
Black man in New York while expanding it for white men. New York's voters were then asked three
times, in 1846, 1860, and 1869, to remove the requirement, and refused each time. It ended only
because of the federal Fifteenth Amendment, and the words were not taken out of the state
constitution until 1874.

constitution's text:

> "but no man of colour, unless he shall have been for three years a citizen of this state, and for
> one year next preceding any election, shall be seized and possessed of a freehold estate of the
> value of two hundred and fifty dollars, over and above all debts and incumbrances charged
> thereon; and shall have been actually rated, and paid a tax thereon, shall be entitled to vote at
> any such election."

**Life domains:** voting. Because the qualification was a property test, it also ties directly to
housing and credit, which is worth a relation in the catalog rather than a domain claim here.

**Posture:** exclusionary.

**Groups reached:** black, white. The clause is one of the clearest cases in the packet of a law
that named both and treated them differently in the same sentence.

## 12. Pennsylvania Constitution of 1838, Article III, Section 1

**Name as the record names it:** Constitution of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania (1838), Article
III, Section 1. Enacting body: Pennsylvania Constitutional Convention of 1837-1838, ratified by
the electorate.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** State (Pennsylvania). Serves the **Northeast** region.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Adopted and signed by the convention | February 22, 1838, at Philadelphia | Constitution's own attestation |
| Ratified by voters | October 9, 1838, 113,971 to 112,759 | Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia; Smith, *Pennsylvania History* (independently gives the same vote totals for October 1838) |
| In force from | January 1, 1839 | Schedule: amendments take effect "from the first day of January, eighteen hundred and thirty-nine" |
| Superseded | 1870, Fifteenth Amendment | Smith, *Pennsylvania History*; Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** Pennsylvania's constitutions of 1776 and 1790 gave the
vote to "freemen" without naming race, and some Black Pennsylvanians voted under them. The 1838
convention inserted one word. Delegates had convened to open the franchise to poor white men who
owned no property; they closed it entirely to Black men, including property owners and taxpayers.
The margin of ratification was about 1,200 votes out of more than 226,000. Black men in
Pennsylvania did not vote again for thirty-two years.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** "white freeman." Article III, Section 1:

> "In elections by the citizens, every white freeman of the age of twenty-one years, having resided
> in the State one year, and in the election district where he offers to vote, ten days immediately
> proceeding such election, and within two years paid a State or county tax ..."

**Life domains:** voting.

**Posture:** exclusionary.

**Groups reached:** black, white.

**Related case worth carrying.** *Hobbs v. Fogg*, 6 Watts 553 (Pa. 1837), decided between the
convention's adoption and the popular ratification, held that free Black men were not "freemen"
entitled to vote under the existing constitution. William Fogg, a Black property owner and
taxpayer in Luzerne County, had been turned away from the polls in October 1835 by elections
inspector Hiram Hobbs, won in the county court, and lost in the Pennsylvania Supreme Court under
Chief Justice Gibson.

## 13. Massachusetts school integration act of 1855

**Name as the record names it:** Chapter 256 of the Massachusetts Acts of 1855, concerning public
schools. Enacting body: Massachusetts General Court.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** State (Massachusetts). Serves the **Northeast** region.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Enacted | April 1855 | Boston College Law Library |
| Effective | September 1, 1855 | Mass Moments teacher resource, Mass Humanities |
| Repealed | never | — |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** Massachusetts became the first state to ban school
segregation by statute, ninety-nine years before *Brown*. The law did two things: it forbade any
distinction by race, color, or religion in deciding who may be admitted to a public school, and it
gave an excluded child a damages action in tort against the city or town that runs the school. The
second half is what gave the first half teeth. The Act was passed to overturn the Massachusetts
Supreme Judicial Court's decision in *Roberts v. City of Boston*, the case in which the "separate
but equal" formula was first upheld, and it came after years of petitioning and a school boycott
led by Black Bostonians including Benjamin Roberts, Robert Morris, and William Cooper Nell.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** The Act names characteristics rather than
groups. Section 1:

> "In determining the qualifications of scholars to be admitted into any public school, or any
> district school in this Commonwealth, no distinction shall be made on account of the race, color,
> or religious opinions, of the applicant or scholar."

Section 2:

> "Any child who, on account of his race, color, or religious opinions, shall be excluded from any
> public or district school in this Commonwealth, for admission to which he may be otherwise
> qualified, shall recover damages therefor, in an action of tort to be brought in the name of said
> child, by his guardian or next friend, in any court of competent jurisdiction to try the same,
> against the city or town by which such school is supported."

**Life domains:** schooling. The damages remedy also touches justice.

**Posture:** protective.

**Groups reached:** all by text; enacted to protect black children.

## 15. Oklahoma Senate Bill One (1907), the Separate Coach Law

**Name as the record names it:** Senate Bill One; known in the Supreme Court's own report as the
"Separate Coach Law." Enacting body: First Oklahoma Legislature. Codified at Rev. Laws, Okla.,
1910, §§860 et seq.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** State (Oklahoma). Serves the **Texas & Oklahoma** region.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Passed Senate 37 to 2, House 91 to 14 | December 1907 | Oklahoma Historical Society |
| Approved | December 18, 1907 | *McCabe v. Atchison, T. & S.F. Ry.*, 235 U.S. 151, 158 (1914): "an act, approved December 18, 1907 (Rev. Laws, Okla., 1910, §§ 860 et seq.), known as the 'Separate Coach Law.'" |
| Effective | sixty days after approval, i.e. about February 16, 1908 | *McCabe*: "The act was to take effect sixty days after its approval (§ 12)." The Court notes suit was filed February 15, 1908, "just before the time when the statute, by its terms, was to become effective." |
| Repealed | 1965 | Oklahoma Historical Society: "the Oklahoma Legislature repealed all segregation statutes for public transportation in 1965" |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** The first bill passed by the first legislature of the new

passengers for hire to provide separate coaches or compartments for white and Black passengers,
and every depot to maintain separate, signed waiting rooms. It defined "negro" to include every

separate facilities faced fines of $100 to $1,000; a passenger who sat in the wrong car or waiting

to be hauled for the exclusive use of one race or the other, "separately but not jointly," which
is precisely the provision the Supreme Court found discriminatory in *McCabe* while still upholding
the statute's core.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** "the white and negro races." As Justice Hughes
quotes the Act in *McCabe*, every railway company doing business in the state as a common carrier
of passengers for hire should

> "provide separate coaches or compartments, for the accommodation of the white and negro races,
> which separate coaches or cars" should "be equal in all points of comfort and convenience"

and at passenger depots there should be maintained "separate waiting rooms," likewise with equal
facilities.

**Life domains:** public_accommodation.

**Posture:** exclusionary.

**Groups reached:** black, white.

## 16. Oklahoma grandfather clause (1910 constitutional amendment)

**Name as the record names it:** The 1910 amendment to Article III, §1 of the Oklahoma
Constitution; called "the so-called Grandfather Clause" by the Supreme Court. Enacting body:
Oklahoma Legislature by referral, adopted by the electorate.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** State (Oklahoma). Serves the **Texas & Oklahoma** region.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Adopted | before November 8, 1910 | *Guinn v. United States*, 238 U.S. 347, 355 (1915): the amendment "was adopted prior to November 8, 1910" |
| First enforced | at the congressional election that followed, November 8, 1910 | *Guinn*, 238 U.S. at 355 |
| Held void | June 21, 1915 | *Guinn v. United States*, 238 U.S. 347 (argued October 17, 1913; decided June 21, 1915) |

**What the rule did, in plain language.** Oklahoma made literacy a condition of registering to
vote, then exempted from the literacy test anyone who had been entitled to vote under any form of
government on January 1, 1866, anyone who lived in a foreign nation on that date, and every lineal
descendant of such a person. January 1, 1866 was chosen because it precedes the Fifteenth
Amendment: on that date no Black person in the former slave states could vote. The exemption
therefore ran to white voters and their descendants and to no one else, without the amendment ever
using a racial word. The Supreme Court held the clause void under the Fifteenth Amendment and held
it inseverable, so the whole amendment fell. Oklahoma responded in 1916 with a registration law
that achieved a similar result, which survived until *Lane v. Wilson* in 1939.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** None. This is the packet's purest example of a
rule that reaches a racial result with no racial word in it. The amendment, as the Court quotes it
at 238 U.S. 357:

> "No person shall be registered as an elector of this State or be allowed to vote in any election
> herein, unless he be able to read and write any section of the constitution of the State of
> Oklahoma; but no person who was, on January 1, 1866, or at any time prior thereto, entitled to
> vote under any form of government, or who at that time resided in some foreign nation, and no
> lineal descendant of such person, shall be denied the right to register and vote because of his
> inability to so read and write sections of such constitution."

**Life domains:** voting.

**Posture:** facially_neutral in its text; the Court's holding is that it operated as a racial
exclusion. If the catalog forces a single value, use exclusionary and record the facial neutrality
in the summary, because the holding is what the timeline is representing.

**Groups reached:** black, white.

## 18. Texas poll tax amendment of 1902

**Name as the record names it:** Amendment to Article VI, Section 2 of the Texas Constitution,
referred as S.J.R. 3 by the 27th Legislature (1901). Enacting body: Texas Legislature by referral,
adopted by the electorate.

**Jurisdiction and reach:** State (Texas). Serves the **Texas & Oklahoma** region.

**In-force window**

| Event | Date | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Referred | S.J.R. 3, 27th Legislature, Regular Session, 1901 | Texas Legislative Reference Library |
| Adopted by voters | November 4, 1902 | Texas Legislative Reference Library |
| Ended for federal elections | Twenty-Fourth Amendment, in effect 1964 | *Handbook of Texas* |
| Ended for state elections | March 24, 1966 | *Harper v. Virginia Bd. of Elections*, 383 U.S. 663 (argued January 25-26, 1966; decided March 24, 1966) |
| Removed from the Texas Constitution | November 8, 1966 | *Handbook of Texas*: "A state constitutional amendment submitted on November 8, 1966, removed the poll tax from the state constitution and embodied a requirement for annual registration." |

The Legislative Reference Library records the amendment's own description as:

> "[Requiring all persons subject to a poll tax to have paid a poll tax before they offer to vote at
> any election in the state, and fixing the time of payment of the poll tax.]"

**What the rule did, in plain language.** Texas made payment of a poll tax a precondition of
voting, and set the payment deadline months before the election, so a voter had to pay in the
winter for an election held in the summer or fall. The tax fell on people with the least cash at
the least convenient moment in the agricultural year. It named nobody, and it removed from the
rolls large numbers of Black voters, Mexican American voters, and poor white voters, which several
courts and the state's own historians record as among its purposes. It outlasted every other rule
in this packet's Texas group, surviving the white primary by more than twenty years.

**Which groups its text names, in its own words.** None. The amendment conditions the franchise on
payment and holding a receipt.

**Life domains:** voting.

**Posture:** facially_neutral. Its disparate effect is well documented and its disfranchising
purpose is asserted by courts, but the amendment's text is a payment requirement.

**Groups reached:** all by text; disparate effect on black, hispanic, and poor white voters.

# DISPUTES

Items that must render as a dispute, with positions named, rather than as a settled finding.

## Dispute 1. Whether the 1935 Social Security Act's exclusion of agricultural and domestic workers was racially motivated

**Applies to:** record #1, Social Security Act of 1935. The same dispute is usually extended to
record #3 (FLSA) and record #2 (NLRA), which carry parallel exclusions.

**What is not in dispute.** The statutory text is settled and quoted above: §§210(b), 811(b), and
907(c) each exclude "Agricultural labor" and "Domestic service in a private home." It is also not
in dispute that those were the occupations in which a large majority of Black workers, and a still
larger majority of Black Southern workers, were employed in 1935, or that the practical result was
that most Black workers accrued no old-age benefit in the program's first fifteen years. The
dispute is about *why* Congress wrote the exclusion, not about what it did.

**Position A: the exclusions were a racial design, driven by Southern congressional power.**

Held by Ira Katznelson, and in related forms by Robert C. Lieberman, Mary Poole, and Jill Quadagno.
Katznelson, writing in his own voice in *Poverty & Race*:

> "Southern members of Congress used occupational exclusions and took advantage of American
> federalism to insure that their region's racial order would not be disturbed by national
> policies."

and

> "Farmworkers and maids, the jobs held by most Southern blacks, were denied Social Security
> pensions and access to labor unions."

Katznelson's larger claim is that the New Deal and Fair Deal statutes together operated as
"affirmative action for whites."

**Position B: the exclusions were about administrative feasibility of tax collection, and the
racial-bias thesis is unsupported.**

Held by Larry DeWitt, then the Social Security Administration's historian, in the agency's own
peer-reviewed bulletin. From the article's abstract, as recorded by the National Library of
Medicine:

> "This article examines both the logic of this thesis and the available empirical evidence on the
> origins of the coverage exclusions. The author concludes that the racial-bias thesis is both
> conceptually flawed and unsupported by the existing empirical evidence. The exclusion of
> agricultural and domestic workers from the early program was due to considerations of
> administrative feasibility involving tax-collection procedures. The author finds no evidence of
> any other policy motive involving racial bias."

Gareth Davies and Martha Derthick argued a similar skeptical case earlier in *Political Science
Quarterly* (1997). That article was not opened for this packet and is named here only as a pointer,
not as a citation.

**Position C: the exclusions were inherited from international precedent, through expert policy
diffusion.**

Held by Richard Rodems and H. Luke Shaefer in *Social Science History*. From the abstract:

> "The exclusion of these categories of workers is consistent with the experience of other
> unemployment insurance programs. Given the close linkages between the technical experts who
> drafted the US legislation and their counterparts abroad, the exclusion of agricultural workers
> and domestic servants from unemployment insurance is best understood as an example of policy
> diffusion."

This is a third position, not a restatement of B: it locates the cause in the drafters' reference
class rather than in either racial intent or American tax administration.

**How the record should carry it.** State what the statute excluded and what the occupational
demography was. Then present A, B, and C as live positions with their holders named. Do not
adjudicate. Note that B is published by the agency that administers the program, which readers
should be able to weigh for themselves.

## Dispute 2. Whether the Fair Labor Standards Act's exclusions were racially motivated

**Applies to:** record #3, Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938.

This is the same three-way dispute as Dispute 1 and should be rendered by reference to it, with one
factual distinction the record should preserve, because it is frequently gotten wrong. FLSA
excluded agricultural workers **expressly**, in §13(a)(6). It did not exclude domestic workers by
name at all. Domestic workers fell outside the Act because the Act's coverage in §§6 and 7 ran only
to employees "engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce," and a private
household is neither. The two exclusions therefore have different mechanisms and different repair
dates: agricultural coverage was broadened effective February 1, 1967, and domestic service was
brought in effective May 1, 1974. Any claim that the 1938 Act "excluded domestic workers" is true in
effect and imprecise as to text.

## Dispute 3. Whether the Servicemen's Readjustment Act belongs in the timeline as exclusionary

**Applies to:** record #4, GI Bill.

**Position A: the GI Bill was in practice a racial wealth transfer to white veterans.** Katznelson
is the best-known proponent and offers the Mississippi loan figure: of 3,229 GI Bill-guaranteed
home, business, and farm loans made in Mississippi in 1947, two went to Black veterans. The
National Archives itself states that Black veterans "were often unable to get bank loans for
mortgages in Black neighborhoods" and faced discrimination that "overwhelming excluded them from
buying homes in 'white' suburban neighborhoods."

**Position B: the statute is facially neutral and the discrimination was committed by banks,
colleges, and state and local administrators rather than by the Act.**

**How the record should carry it.** The statute's posture is facially_neutral, verifiably: a
full-text search of the Act returns no racial word. The contested question is whether a benefit
delivered entirely through discriminating institutions should be classified by its text or by its
administration. The record should set the posture as facially_neutral and carry the dispute about
its effect, rather than reclassify the statute. Note that the Mississippi loan figure comes from
Katznelson and was read in his *Poverty & Race* essay; it has not been traced here to the
underlying VA records.

## Dispute 4. Whether the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 should be classified as protective

**Applies to:** record #6.

The Act abolished the national-origins quota system, which is unambiguously the removal of a racial
and national-origin allocation, and it added an express antidiscrimination rule for visa issuance.
In the same statute Congress imposed the first numerical ceiling ever placed on Western Hemisphere
immigration, 120,000 a year beginning in fiscal 1968 under §21(e), which restricted Mexican
migration that had previously been numerically unlimited. Whether the Act reads as protective or as
a trade depends on which population is in view. The record should carry both operative facts from
the statute's own text and let the posture field say protective with the ceiling noted, rather than
suppressing one half.

## Dispute 5. Whether IRCA's employer sanctions caused discrimination against authorized minority workers

**Applies to:** record #7.

Congress itself anticipated the risk, which is why §102 created the unfair-immigration-related
employment practice provision at 8 U.S.C. §1324b in the same Act. Whether the sanctions in fact
produced measurable hiring discrimination against authorized workers who appeared foreign, and
whether §1324b was adequate to check it, is contested. The General Accounting Office studied the
question in the years after enactment. No GAO report was opened for this packet, so the record
should state the dispute exists and that Congress legislated against the risk in advance, without
asserting a finding on magnitude. This item is flagged as a dispute chiefly so it is not authored
as a settled finding in either direction.
