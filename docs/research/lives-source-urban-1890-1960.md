# Urban residence by race, by state, 1890-1960: published sources

**Status:** Research record, read-only. Nothing in the project was changed.
**Date:** 2026-09-15
**Gap addressed:** `urban` condition, 1890 / 1900 / 1910 / 1920 / 1930 / 1940 / 1950 / 1960, for the
national baseline and the six regions. Eight decades, seven areas, three groups.

## Outcome first

The gap is closeable with published Census Bureau tables at **state level for all eight decades**, for
Black and white. No academic reconstruction is needed. Nothing has to change in
`docs/methodology/lives-across-decades.md`: every table below is a Census Bureau publication and
stores as `observed`.

The work splits cleanly into four sources:

| Decades | Source | Geography | Both races? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1890, 1900, 1910 | 1910 Census, Vol. I, Ch. II, **Table 42**, pp. 189-195 | all states + DC | yes |
| 1910, 1920, 1930, 1940 | 1940 Census, Vol. II, state chapters, **Table 5** | all states + DC | yes |
| 1950 | 1950 Census, Vol. II, state chapters, **Table 14** | all states + DC | yes |
| 1960 | 1960 Census, Vol. I, state chapters, **Table 15** | all states + DC | yes |

1910 appears in two of them, which gives a free cross-check (verified below).

Hispanic is not closeable and never was. No table in any of these volumes carries a Spanish-origin
concept, which matches what the NHGIS map already records.

## Sources opened, in order of usefulness

### 1. 1910 Census, Volume I, Table 42 — the single best find

**Citation.** U.S. Bureau of the Census, *Thirteenth Census of the United States Taken in the Year
1910. Volume I: Population 1910, General Report and Analysis* (Washington: GPO, 1913), Chapter II,
"Color or Race, Nativity, and Parentage."

**URL opened.** <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1910/volume-1/36894832v1ch03.pdf>
(chapter file; Chapter II begins at book p. 125 = PDF p. 1, so book p. 189 = PDF p. 65).
Landing page: <https://www.census.gov/library/publications/1913/dec/vol-1-population.html>

**Table and pages.** Table 42, "COLOR OR RACE, NATIVITY, AND PARENTAGE IN URBAN AND RURAL COMMUNITIES,
BY DIVISIONS AND STATES: 1910, 1900, AND 1890," pp. 189-195. Page 189 carries the United States total
and the nine geographic divisions; the state rows run from p. 190 (Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, …)
through p. 195. I opened pp. 189 and 190 and read the rows.

**Shape.** Columns are NUMBER (1910 Urban / Rural, 1900 Urban / Rural, 1890 Urban / Rural) and PER CENT
(same six). Rows per area: Total; White; Negro; "Indian, Chinese, Japanese, and all other"; then Native
white (native parentage / foreign or mixed parentage); Foreign-born white. Counts **and** percentages
are both printed, so nothing has to be divided.

**Geography.** State level, all states plus DC, plus divisions and the nation. All seven project areas
unlock from this one table.

**Census years.** 1890, 1900, 1910.

**Urban definition.** Not restated in the table headnote (which reads only "[Per cent not shown where
base is less than 100.]"). It is the 2,500-inhabitant rule; the companion 1918 volume (source 2) states
it in words, and its Table 9 reproduces Table 42's Negro columns digit for digit, so the same rule
governs all three years here.

**Race vocabulary.** "White," "Negro," "Indian, Chinese, Japanese, and all other." **Black-only** on the
Negro line. Not an aggregate. This is the good case, not the trap.

**Tier.** **A.** A published Census Bureau table in a decennial volume.

**Closes.** 3 of 8 decades, at state level, for both Black and white, with one table.

Sanity figures read off p. 189 (United States): 1910 Negro urban 2,689,229 / rural 7,138,534; 1900
2,005,972 / 6,828,022; 1890 1,481,142 / 6,007,534. White 1890 urban 21,173,685 / rural 33,927,573.
Percent urban: Negro 27.4 / 22.7 / 19.8, White 48.7 / 43.0 / 38.4.

### 2. *Negro Population in the United States, 1790-1915* (1918)

**Citation.** U.S. Bureau of the Census, *Negro Population in the United States, 1790-1915*
(Washington: GPO, 1918), Chapter VII, "Urbanization: Urban and Rural Classification, and Population of
Cities."

**URL opened.** <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1910/black-population-1790-1915/00480330ch03.pdf>
(chapter file covering book pp. 61-144; book p. 91 = PDF p. 32, p. 92 = PDF p. 33).
Landing page: <https://www.census.gov/library/publications/1918/dec/negro-population-1790-1915.html>

**Tables and pages.**
- **Table 8, p. 91** — "POPULATION: 1910," Urban (Negro, White), Rural (Negro, White), Percentage urban
  (Negro, White), by division and state. 1910 only.
- **Table 9, p. 92** — "NEGROES IN URBAN AND RURAL COMMUNITIES, BY DIVISIONS AND STATES: 1910, 1900, AND
  1890." Negro urban and rural counts for all three years, percent urban and rural, and percent Negro in
  the total urban and rural population, by division and state.
- Table 2, p. 88 — national urban/rural by racial class for 1910, 1900, 1890 (nation only).
- Table 5, p. 90 — percent urban and rural, Negro population, by section and division, 1910/1900/1890.

I opened pp. 91 and 92 and read both tables.

**Geography.** State level, all states plus DC, plus divisions and sections.

**Census years.** 1890, 1900, 1910 (Table 9); 1910 only for the white comparison (Table 8).

**Urban definition, in the volume's words** (p. 87): "In the census tabulations the population living in
cities and towns of 2,500 or more inhabitants is classified as urban, and the population living in
smaller villages and towns and in open country districts as rural." Table 9's own headnote repeats it:
"[Urban includes population living in places of 2,500 or more inhabitants; rural includes all other.]"
The volume also warns that the classification "relates … to an arbitrarily determined population limit"
and does not capture small-village population.

**Race vocabulary.** "Negro" and "White," with White further split into native (native parentage, mixed
parentage, foreign parentage) and foreign-born. **Black-only.**

**Footnote worth carrying.** Oklahoma's row is footnoted "Includes population of Indian Territory for
1900 and 1890."

**Tier.** **A.**

**Closes.** The same 3 decades as source 1, Black-only for 1890 and 1900. **Use it as the
corroborating second source, not the primary**, because Table 42 gives the white side for 1890 and 1900
and this volume does not.

### 3. 1940 Census, Volume II, state chapters, Table 5 — the workhorse for 1910-1940

**Citation.** U.S. Bureau of the Census, *Sixteenth Census of the United States: 1940. Population.
Volume II: Characteristics of the Population* (Washington: GPO, 1943).

**URLs opened.**
- Part 1, United States Summary, Chapter 2:
  <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1940/population-volume-2/33973538v2p1ch2.pdf>
  (book p. 20 = PDF p. 16).
- Part 2 contents (lists the 34 standard state tables):
  <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1940/population-volume-2/33973538v2p2.pdf>
- Part 2 tables (Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa):
  <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1940/population-volume-2/33973538v2p2ch2.pdf>
  (Florida's Table 5 is book p. 15 = PDF p. 5).
- Directory of all seven parts:
  <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1940/population-volume-2/>

**Tables and pages.**
- **US Summary Table 5, p. 20** — "URBAN, RURAL-NONFARM, AND RURAL-FARM POPULATION, BY RACE, NATIVITY,
  AND SEX, FOR THE UNITED STATES: 1910 TO 1940." Opened and read.
- **State Table 5** (same title, "FOR THE STATE") — one per state, in a series of 34 standard tables
  repeated identically in every state chapter. I opened Florida's, book p. 15.

**Shape.** Rows: URBAN / RURAL-NONFARM / RURAL-FARM, each split Total / Native / Foreign born, and each
repeated for Total, Male, Female, for census years 1940, 1930, 1920, 1910. Columns: All classes, White,
**Negro**, Other races, plus percent by race, percent by nativity, and males per 100 females.

**Geography.** State level for every state plus DC (Volume II runs Part 1 = US Summary and Parts 2-7
covering the states alphabetically). The US Summary volume itself is **not** enough: its state tables
(21 population urban/rural, 22 population by race, 23 race by sex) put residence and race on separate
axes, exactly the NHGIS failure mode. The crossing lives only in the state chapters.

**Census years.** 1910, 1920, 1930, 1940 — four decades in one table.

**Urban definition.** The 1940 definition (incorporated places of 2,500+, plus the special New England
town and dense-township rules adopted in 1930). Earlier years are reprinted as they were published, not
restated: Florida's 1930 Negro urban figure here (210,292), 1920 (120,596) and 1910 (88,586) match
*Negroes in the United States, 1920-32* Table 8 exactly.

**Race vocabulary.** "White," "Negro," "Other races." **Black-only.**

**Trap in the headnote, and it is a real one.** "[Figures for white population in 1930 have been revised
to include Mexicans who were classified with 'Other races' in the 1930 reports.]" So this volume's 1930
**white** urban and rural figures are **not** the same as the 1930-published or 1935-published white
figures. Both are Census Bureau publications. The project has to pick one vintage per decade and say
which, or the 1930 white line will move depending on which volume was transcribed.

**Note for ingest.** There is no single "Rural" row. Rural = rural-nonfarm + rural-farm, a sum of two
published lines, which under the method is `derived` unless the rule is relaxed for pure addition.

**Tier.** **A.**

**Closes.** 4 of 8 decades (1910, 1920, 1930, 1940) at state level for both races.

### 4. *Negroes in the United States, 1920-32* (1935)

**Citation.** U.S. Bureau of the Census, *Negroes in the United States, 1920-32*, prepared under the
supervision of Z. R. Pettet by Charles E. Hall (Washington: GPO, 1935), Chapter "Urbanization."

**URL opened.** <https://archive.org/details/negroesinuniteds00unit> — PDF read at
<https://ia800809.us.archive.org/8/items/negroesinuniteds00unit/negroesinuniteds00unit.pdf>
(book p. 52 = PDF p. 78, p. 53 = PDF p. 79). **Not on census.gov.** I checked the 1930 decennial
publications index and the census.gov search; the only Black-population special report census.gov hosts
for 1930 is *The Negro Farmer in the United States*. Citing this volume means citing a scan of a Census
Bureau publication held by the Internet Archive.

**Tables and pages.** Both opened and read.
- **Table 7, p. 52** — "NEGRO AND WHITE POPULATION, URBAN AND RURAL, BY SECTIONS, DIVISIONS, AND STATES:
  1930." Urban (Negro, White), Rural (Negro, White), Percent urban (Negro, White).
- **Table 8, p. 53** — "NEGRO POPULATION, URBAN AND RURAL, BY SECTIONS, DIVISIONS, AND STATES; 1930,
  1920, AND 1910." Urban and rural counts for all three years, plus percent urban/rural and Negro
  percentage of total urban/rural.

**Geography.** State level, all states plus DC, plus divisions and sections.

**Census years.** 1910, 1920, 1930.

**Urban definition, in the volume's words** (Introduction, p. xv-xvi): urban is "in general, that
residing in cities or other incorporated places having 2,500 inhabitants or more, the remainder being
classified as rural," with the New Hampshire / Massachusetts / Rhode Island town rule tightened in 1930
and a new rule extending urban to unincorporated townships of 10,000+ with density of 1,000+ per square
mile. And the warning the project needs verbatim: "In the tables in this volume, the figures for 1910
and 1920 are presented as these statistics were then published, hence the data for these 2 years are not
exactly comparable with those for 1930 for urban and rural areas."

**Race vocabulary.** "Negro" and "White." **Black-only.**

**Tier.** **A** by content (a Census Bureau volume), with the access caveat that the only copy I could
open is an Internet Archive scan.

**Closes.** 3 decades that source 3 already closes from a census.gov-hosted volume. **Value is as the
second source and as the definition note**, not as the primary. Its 1930 white figures are the
pre-revision ones (no Mexican reclassification), which is the other side of the source-3 trap.

### 5. 1950 Census, Volume II, state chapters, Table 14

**Citation.** U.S. Bureau of the Census, *United States Census of Population: 1950. Volume II:
Characteristics of the Population* (Washington: GPO, 1952).

**URLs opened.**
- Part 1, US Summary, Chapter B:
  <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1950/population-volume-2/21983999v2p1ch3.pdf>
  (Table 60 at book p. 1-107 = PDF p. 23).
- Part 2, Alabama, Chapter B:
  <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1950/population-volume-2/37778831v2p2ch3.pdf>
  (Tables 13 and 14 at book p. 2-30 = PDF p. 6).
- Directory of all parts:
  <https://www2.census.gov/library/publications/decennial/1950/population-volume-2/>

**Tables and pages.**
- **State Table 14** — "RACE BY SEX, FOR THE STATE, URBAN AND RURAL, 1950, AND FOR THE STATE, 1880 TO
  1940," book p. 2-30 in the Alabama chapter. Rows for 1950: TOTAL / Urban / Rural nonfarm / Rural farm.
  Columns: All classes, White (Total, Native, Foreign born), **Negro**, Other races (Indian, Japanese,
  Chinese, All other), and percent of total. Opened and read.
  The 1880-1940 rows in the same table are **state totals only, with no urban/rural cross**, so this
  table closes 1950 and nothing earlier.
- **State Table 13** — "COLOR BY SEX, FOR THE STATE, URBAN AND RURAL: 1930 TO 1950," same page. White /
  **Nonwhite** by Urban, Urban farm, Rural, Rural nonfarm, Rural farm, for 1950 under **both** the new
  and old urban definitions, and for 1940 and 1930. Opened and read.
- **US Summary Table 60**, p. 1-107 — "COLOR AND SEX, BY REGIONS, DIVISIONS, AND STATES, URBAN AND
  RURAL: 1950." This is the trap: state level, but White / **Nonwhite** only. Opened and read.

**Geography.** State level for every state plus DC, one chapter per state.

**Census years.** 1950.

**Urban definition.** The 1950 urbanized-area definition ("new"). Table 13 proves the size of the break:
Alabama urban, all classes, 1,340,937 on the new definition against 1,228,209 on the old, a 9 percent
step. Table 14's 1950 urban line (1,340,937 all classes) is the **new** definition only, so a Black
figure taken from Table 14 is on the new basis with no old-basis counterpart. The break between 1940 and
1950 is therefore definitional as well as real, and the count note has to say so.

**Race vocabulary.** Table 14 is "Race": White, **Negro**, Other races. **Black-only.** Table 13 and US
Summary Table 60 are "Color": White / **Nonwhite**, an all-non-white aggregate. Both words appear on the
same printed page. Taking the wrong one publishes a wrong figure.

**Tier.** **A.**

**Closes.** 1 of 8 decades at state level for both races.

## The trap, concretely

Every decade from 1940 on prints "Negro" and "Nonwhite" on adjacent pages, sometimes on the same page.

| Decade | State x urban/rural, Black-only | State x urban/rural, aggregate |
|---|---|---|
| 1940 | Vol. II state Table 5 — White / **Negro** / Other races | — |
| 1950 | Vol. II state Table 14 — White / **Negro** / Other races | Vol. II state Table 13 and US Summary Table 60 — White / **Nonwhite** |
| 1960 | Vol. I state Table 15 — White / Nonwhite / **Negro** / Other races | Vol. I US Summary Table 58 — White / **Nonwhite** |

In 1960 the difference is not cosmetic. From Table 51 (regions), the West's urban Nonwhite is 1,752,322
against urban Negro 1,006,046 — Nonwhite is 74 percent larger. In the Deep South states the two are
close; in the project's West and in Oklahoma, Arizona and New Mexico they are not. Storing a Nonwhite
figure under the `black` slice would be wrong by a factor approaching two in one whole region.

Second trap, a definitional one: the 1940 volume's **white** 1930 figures include Mexicans, who the 1930
and 1935 volumes classified with "Other races." Two Census Bureau publications, two different 1930 white
urban counts. Pick a vintage per decade and record it.

## Effort estimate for the transcription

- 1890 / 1900 / 1910: **one table, seven pages** (1910 Vol. I, pp. 189-195). One PDF.
- 1910 / 1920 / 1930 / 1940: **one table per state**, in six part-volumes (1940 Vol. II Parts 2-7). Six
  PDFs to fetch; about 49 table instances to read.
- 1950: **one table per state** (1950 Vol. II Parts 2-52). About 50 PDFs.
- 1960: **one table per state** (1960 Vol. I Parts 2-52, Chapter B files). About 50 PDFs.

The 1890-1940 half is cheap. The 1950 and 1960 halves are the volume work, and both are one table number
repeated identically across state chapters, so a single extraction routine covers each.

## RECOMMENDATION

**Take the four-source path above, and start with the 1910 Volume I Table 42.**

Reasoning, in order:

1. **Table 42 alone closes three of the eight decades for both races, at state level, from one
   census.gov-hosted PDF, with counts and percentages already printed.** Nothing else found comes close
   on effort per cell. Do it first and the timeline gains 1890, 1900 and 1910 across all seven areas in a
   single pass.
2. **Then the 1940 Volume II state Table 5**, which closes 1920, 1930 and 1940 (and re-closes 1910 as a
   free check) from six PDFs. That takes the gap from eight decades to two.
3. **Then 1950 and 1960**, which are the same shape of work repeated per state and can be batched.
4. **Load Historical Statistics Series A 73-81 as the national row for all eight decades first**, before
   any state work, because it is one page and it gives an immediate arithmetic check on every state sum
   you later produce, plus the definition labels the count notes need.

Use the 1918 and 1935 retrospective volumes as **corroborating second sources and as the source of the
definition language**, not as primaries. They are Black-only where Table 42 and Table 5 carry white too,
and the 1935 volume is not hosted by census.gov.

Everything stores as `observed`, except: a 1940 or 1950 rural figure, which is rural-nonfarm plus
rural-farm and is therefore `derived`; and a Historical Statistics race total, which is male plus female
and is therefore `derived`.

**Three count notes this work will require:**
- **1950 urban definition break.** Urbanized areas enter in 1950. The 1950 state Table 13 prints both
  bases for white and nonwhite, so the size of the break can be stated per state, even though the Black
  figure itself exists only on the new basis. Alabama's step was 9 percent.
- **1930 urban rule change.** New England town rule tightened and dense unincorporated townships added,
  per the 1935 volume's own words, so 1910 and 1920 "are not exactly comparable" with 1930.
- **1930 white, two vintages.** The 1940 volume moved Mexicans into white for 1930; the 1930 and 1935
  volumes did not.

## DEAD ENDS

**Historical Statistics, Series A 195-209, "Population of States, by Sex, Race, Urban-Rural Residence,
and Age: 1790 to 1970"** (same PDF, p. 25). Opened and read. The title promises exactly the target and
the table does not deliver it: race (White, Negro, Other races) and residence (Urban, Rural) are **two
separate column groups on one row**, never crossed. Per state per decade you get white, Negro, urban and
rural as four independent totals. This is the identical failure mode that ruled out the NHGIS tables, in
the one compendium most likely to be reached for first. Worth recording so nobody opens it twice.

**1940 Census, Volume II, Part 1 (US Summary)** — the state tables there are Table 21 (population urban
and rural by state, no race), Table 22 (population by race by state, no residence) and Table 23 (race by
sex by state, no residence). Race and residence on separate axes again. The crossing exists only in the
state chapters. Read from the volume's own contents list, book p. VII.

**1950 Census, Volume II, Part 1 (US Summary), Chapter B.** Scanned the full table list. The only
state-level table crossing residence with any race concept is Table 60, and it is White / Nonwhite.
Table 36 ("Race by sex, for the United States, urban and rural, 1950, and for the United States, 1850 to
1940") is national only.

## Was anything genuinely never published this way?

No. For this condition, every decade 1890 through 1960 was published crossed by race at state level, in
the volumes above. The gap is an access gap, not a publication gap. Two qualifications:

- **Hispanic, 1890-1960: genuinely never published.** No Spanish-origin concept exists in any of these
  tables. This is a real content gap and the timeline should keep saying so.
- **"Other races" is not a usable third group.** In these tables it means Indian, Chinese, Japanese,
  Filipino and "all other" combined, and in 1930 it excludes Mexicans in some volumes and includes them
  in others.
